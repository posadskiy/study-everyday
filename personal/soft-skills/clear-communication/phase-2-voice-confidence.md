# Phase 2 — Voice & Confidence (Months 4–6)

**Goal:** Speak under pressure without freezing. Build comfort speaking in both English and Russian.

**Time commitment:** ~3 hours per week + daily 5-minute recording habit

**What you will be able to do by the end of this phase:**
Deliver a 5–7 minute prepared talk in your stronger language. Give a comfortable 60-second impromptu answer in both languages on any unfamiliar topic. Use the TALK framework consciously in real conversations.

---

## Why This Phase Comes Second

In Phase 1 you learned to structure your thoughts. Now the problem shifts: you can structure a thought on paper, but can you deliver it under pressure — when someone is watching, when you are speaking in a second language, when you are put on the spot?

Most people have no problem communicating when they are relaxed and prepared. The gap appears in three specific situations:
1. You are asked an unexpected question in a meeting
2. You need to speak in front of a group
3. You are speaking in a language that is not your first

This phase is about closing that gap. The mechanism is simple but requires repetition: you expose yourself to the uncomfortable situation repeatedly, in progressively higher-stakes versions, until your nervous system learns that it is safe. There is no shortcut.

---

## Understanding the Anxiety Problem

Before starting the drills, it helps to understand what is actually happening when you freeze or ramble under pressure.

When you feel anxiety before or during speaking, your brain interprets the situation as a threat. The same physiological response that prepares you to run from danger — increased heart rate, shallow breathing, adrenaline — is being triggered by a conversation. This is not a personality flaw. It is a wiring problem that is fully correctable through repeated exposure.

**The counterintuitive insight from research (Alison Wood Brooks, Harvard):**
When you feel nervous before speaking, do not try to calm down. Instead, tell yourself "I'm excited." This is not positive thinking — it is a physiological reframe. Anxiety and excitement produce identical physical states. The only difference is the label. Labeling it excitement (rather than calm) produces significantly better speaking performance in controlled experiments, because it puts you in a high-arousal positive state rather than fighting against your own nervous system.

Try it before your next recording or real talk. Say out loud: "I'm excited." Then start.

## Books for This Phase

### Book 1: *Think Faster, Talk Smarter* — Matt Abrahams (Stanford)
**Read this first in Phase 2.**

Abrahams teaches Strategic Communication at Stanford's Graduate School of Business. This book is specifically about spontaneous communication — the moments when you have no preparation time. Unlike most communication books that focus on presentations and formal speeches, this one focuses on the moments that actually happen most: being put on the spot, answering questions on the fly, making small talk, handling difficult questions.

What you will learn:
- Why spontaneous communication feels harder than it is, and what to do about it
- How to manage anxiety in real time using science-backed techniques
- How to structure impromptu answers using PREP and other frameworks
- How to make your answers shorter without feeling rushed
- Why listening matters as much as speaking

**Key insight from the book:** Most people prepare what they want to say. The better approach is to prepare how you will listen and respond. A well-structured response to what the person actually asked is worth more than a rehearsed answer to what you assumed they would ask.

### Book 2: *Talk: The Science of Conversation* — Alison Wood Brooks (Harvard, 2026)
**Read second, or read in parallel with Abrahams.**

This is the most current and research-grounded book on conversation available in 2026. Brooks spent years studying what makes conversations go well or badly, and the results are often surprising — they challenge many common assumptions about what "good communication" looks like.

What you will learn:
- The TALK framework: Topics, Asking, Levity, Kindness
- Why asking more questions consistently produces better conversations (and why people instinctively ask fewer than they should)
- What levity actually does in a conversation and why it is not trivial
- The difference between listening to respond and listening to understand — and how to develop the second kind

**Key insight from the book:** The quality of a conversation is determined more by how much you are genuinely interested in the other person than by how interesting you are yourself. Curiosity is a communication skill.

## Join Toastmasters — The Single Highest-ROI Action This Phase

**Do this in the first two weeks of Phase 2.**

Toastmasters is a non-profit organization with clubs in virtually every city and country. Meetings happen weekly or bi-weekly. At each meeting, members give short prepared speeches and impromptu one-to-two minute talks. Everything is done in a supportive environment where the goal is learning, not judging.

Why it is the highest ROI action:
- It gives you a real audience, not just a phone camera. Your nervous system cannot fake this.
- It provides structured feedback after every talk.
- It has a graduated program: speeches get progressively longer and more complex as you improve.
- It meets regularly — which forces you to practice on a schedule rather than when you feel like it.
- It costs almost nothing (small annual membership fee).

**How to find a club:**
- Go to toastmasters.org/find-a-club
- Filter by language (search for both English and Russian language clubs)
- Visit two or three clubs as a guest before joining — clubs have different cultures and you want one that fits
- Commit to attending 2x per month minimum

**If Toastmasters is not available in your area:**
- Online Toastmasters clubs exist and work well
- English Speaking Clubs (search locally or on Meetup.com)
- A conversation exchange partner via Tandem or HelloTalk

## The Daily Habit for Phase 2

**Replace the Phase 1 micro-habit with this:**

Every day, record yourself speaking for 60 seconds on any topic. Any topic. What you ate, what you are working on, what you are reading. In English and Russian alternating days.

Rules:
- No script, no preparation
- Speak in complete sentences, not bullet points
- Do not stop and restart. Record from start to finish.
- Every Sunday: watch the week's recordings. Pick the best and worst. Write what was different.

After 90 days of this, you will have made 90 recordings. Your speaking fluency, your sentence structure, and your comfort with the camera will all improve measurably — not because of any single breakthrough, but because of repetition.

### Week 14

**Theme: TALK framework — the asking habit**

This week you focus on one element of the TALK framework: Asking.

Read the TALK section in [frameworks.md](frameworks.md) before starting the week.

**The experiment this week:**
In every conversation you have at work or at home, ask at least one follow-up question before introducing your own view. Just one. "What happened next?" or "How did you decide that?" or "What made it difficult?"

Track how conversations change when you ask more. Most people find that conversations become richer and that the other person becomes more engaged. This is what Brooks' research shows consistently.

**Reflection prompt for Sunday:**
Did asking more questions feel unnatural? Did it change the tone of any conversation? Write 5 lines.

---

### Week 15

**Theme: The pause and the voice**

Two specific physical habits to develop this week.

**The pause:**
In every answer you give to any question this week, pause for 1–2 seconds before you start speaking. This is harder than it sounds. Silence feels much longer to you than to the listener. Practice tolerating it.

Why this matters: The pause signals that you are thinking rather than reacting. It makes you appear more confident and considered even before you say anything. It also gives your brain a moment to organize the answer using PREP before the words start coming out.

**The voice:**
Record one of your daily 60-second talks and pay attention only to your voice — not the content. Ask:
- Are you speaking too fast? (Most people speed up when nervous)
- Is your volume dropping at the end of sentences?
- Are you ending statements with an upward inflection, as if asking a question?
- Are you speaking in a monotone?

Pick one of these to fix this week. Just one. Record again at the end of the week and compare.

### Weeks 16–18 — Toastmasters Preparation

**Prepare and deliver your first Toastmasters speech.**

Every Toastmaster gives a "Icebreaker" speech as their first: a 4–6 minute talk introducing themselves. This is deliberately low-stakes because everyone remembers being new.

**Preparation process:**
1. Decide on 3 things you want people to know about you. These become the 3 body points of a Pyramid structure.
2. Write an opening that gets attention — a question, a surprising fact, a short story.
3. Write a conclusion that ties back to the opening.
4. Memorize the structure, not the words. You should know what comes next, not what to say word for word.
5. Practice it 5 times before delivering. Each practice should be spoken aloud, not in your head.

**A note on memorization:**
Memorizing word-for-word makes your delivery robotic and means that any deviation from the script causes panic. Memorizing the structure means you always know where you are and where you are going, and the words can vary naturally. This is how experienced speakers work.

## Common Mistakes in Phase 2

### Mistake 1: Practicing only in private
Recording yourself is good. Speaking only to your phone camera is not enough. Your nervous system responds differently to a real audience — there are real eyes on you, real silence when you pause, real reactions. Toastmasters or any live group is not optional. It is the mechanism of the change.

### Mistake 2: Focusing on eliminating filler words through willpower
The solution to "um" and "uh" is not to try harder to suppress them. It is to replace them with a pause. When your brain needs time to find the next word, instead of filling the silence with "um," pause. Silence is more powerful than filler. Practice the pause first; the filler words disappear as a result.

### Mistake 3: Preparing speeches word by word
Knowing exactly what you want to say, word for word, works in one specific scenario: you deliver it perfectly. In every other scenario — a question interrupts you, you lose your place, the time changes — you are in trouble because you have nothing to fall back on. Prepare the structure. Let the words be free.

### Mistake 4: Treating the language gap as the main problem
Speaking clearly in a second language is harder than in your first. But most of the problems — rambling, losing structure, not knowing how to end a sentence — exist in your first language too. Fix the structure first. The language barrier becomes much smaller once you have a clear shape for your thoughts.

### Mistake 5: Skipping the TALK framework because it feels artificial
Preparing topics before conversations feels awkward. Consciously asking more questions feels forced. This feeling of artificiality means you are doing something new — not that you are doing something wrong. The framework becomes natural after a few weeks of deliberate use.
