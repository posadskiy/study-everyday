# Phase 1 — Structure & Brevity (Months 1–3)

**Goal:** Stop rambling. Learn to structure thoughts before you speak or write.

**Time commitment:** ~3 hours per week + a 5-minute daily micro-habit

**What you will be able to do by the end of this phase:**
Deliver a 3-minute structured update on any work topic — clear opening, 3 supporting points, a concrete ask — with no rambling and no filler words.

---

## Why This Phase Comes First

Before you work on confidence, listening, or handling conflict, you need to be able to structure a thought. Everything else builds on this.

Here is the real problem: **most people communicate in the order they think, not in the order the listener needs.** When you are figuring something out, your brain naturally moves from context → evidence → conclusion. That is fine for thinking. It is terrible for communicating.

Your listener does not want to follow your thinking process. They want the answer first, then the reasons if they need them. The frameworks in this phase teach you to flip the order — to always lead with the point.

This is uncomfortable at first because it feels abrupt. You will feel like you are skipping important context. You are not. You are just moving it to second place instead of first.

---

## Books for This Phase

### Book 1: *On Writing Well* — William Zinsser
**Read this one first. Read it cover to cover.**

This book was written for journalists but it is the most practical guide to clear thinking through clear writing that exists. Zinsser's argument is simple: clutter is the enemy of communication. Every unnecessary word is a tax on the reader's attention. Every sentence that could be shorter should be shorter.

What you will learn:
- How to identify and cut "clutter words" — words that add length but not meaning
- Why short sentences almost always work better than long ones
- How to write in your own voice rather than "professional" language that sounds like a robot
- Why rewriting matters more than writing

Key chapters to prioritize: 1 (The Transaction), 2 (Simplicity), 3 (Clutter), 7 (Words).

**One thing to do while reading:** Underline every sentence you wish you had written. At the end of the book, reread those sentences and notice what they have in common.

### Book 2: *The Pyramid Principle* — Barbara Minto
**Read after Zinsser. You can skim the later chapters — the first 5 are the core.**

This book is denser and more technical, but it gives you something Zinsser does not: a concrete structure for organizing complex information. Minto developed this framework at McKinsey to help consultants write better documents. It is now used across consulting, banking, tech, and any field that requires structured thinking.

What you will learn:
- How to organize any document so the conclusion comes first
- How to group arguments so they are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive (MECE) — meaning no overlaps and no gaps
- How to test whether your structure makes logical sense

You do not need to memorize the whole book. Understand the pyramid shape and practice it. Everything else in the book is refinement.

## The Daily Micro-Habit (Start Today — 5 Minutes)

This is the single most important thing in Phase 1. Do it every day from Day 1.

**Before sending any Slack message or email:**
1. Write it the way it comes out naturally
2. Find the main point — what do you need the reader to do or know?
3. Move that point to the first sentence
4. Cut any word that does not add meaning (read each sentence and ask: "does removing this word change the meaning?" If no, remove it)
5. Send

The goal is not perfection. The goal is the habit. After 30 days of this, BLUF will become your default and you will not need to think about it.

### Week 1

**Theme: Orientation and baseline**

The goal this week is to get set up, capture your current baseline, and begin the daily habit. No pressure to be good yet. You are just starting.

#### Day 1 — Setup
- Subscribe to *Think Fast, Talk Smart* podcast by Matt Abrahams (free, Apple Podcasts / Spotify)
- Order or download *On Writing Well* by William Zinsser
- Listen to one episode of Think Fast, Talk Smart — search for episodes on structure, brevity, or clarity. Episode "How to Speak Concisely" is a good start.
- After listening: write 5 lines in a note. What was the main idea? What one thing will you try tomorrow?

#### Day 2 — Baseline recording
This is important. Record yourself so you can compare Month 1 to Month 12.

- Pick any topic you know well — a work project, a technology you use, a decision you made recently
- Set a timer for 3 minutes
- Record yourself on your phone camera talking about this topic. No preparation, no script.
- When done: watch it back once. Write three observations:
  - One thing that was okay
  - One thing that was unclear or rambling
  - One filler word or phrase you used repeatedly ("basically", "you know", "kind of", "so yeah")
- Save the file. Label it "Month 1 baseline." You will compare it in Month 12.

Do not cringe. The point is not to be good. The point is to have a starting point.

#### Day 3 — First email rewrite + start daily habit
- Find an email or Slack message you sent this week
- Rewrite it applying BLUF: move the main point to the first sentence, cut all context that comes before it
- Count the words in the original and the rewrite. Note the difference.
- **Start the daily micro-habit today.** Every message you send from now on: BLUF + cut 30%.

### Week 2

**Theme: Learn and apply BLUF**

#### Monday — Reading (30 min)
Read Chapters 1–3 of *On Writing Well*:
- Chapter 1: The Transaction — why clarity is a gift to your reader
- Chapter 2: Simplicity — the direct connection between clear thinking and clear writing
- Chapter 3: Clutter — how to identify and remove words that do nothing

After reading, write one sentence: what is the single most useful thing you read today?

#### Tuesday — Writing drill: BLUF practice (45 min)
**The drill:**
1. Pick a real topic from your work — a decision you are thinking about, a project you are working on, a problem you have noticed
2. Write a 200-word summary of it the natural way — just get it out
3. Now find the main point. Ask: "If someone reads one sentence about this, what should it be?"
4. Rewrite: move that sentence to the top. Let the rest follow.
5. Count words. Cut to 150 words without losing meaning.

What to pay attention to:
- The first sentence should stand alone. If it contains the word "regarding" or "about," rewrite it.
- Look for sentences that begin with "As you know..." or "Background:" — these are almost always postponing the point. Delete them or move them below the main point.

#### Wednesday — Podcast + reflection (30 min)
- Listen to one episode of Think Fast, Talk Smart
- Suggested: any episode on "concise communication" or "spontaneous speaking"
- After: write 5 lines. What was the idea? How does it connect to what you already read? What will you try?

#### Thursday — Recording practice (60 min)
- Pick a different topic from last week's recording
- Set 5 minutes this time (longer than last week)
- Record yourself explaining the topic as clearly as you can
- Watch it back. Try to apply BLUF: did you state the main point in the first 20 seconds? If not, note where the point finally appeared.
- Compare to last week's recording — any difference in how you started?

#### Friday — Email rewrite (15 min)
Same as Week 1. Pick today's most important outgoing message. Apply BLUF. Cut 30%.

#### Sunday — Week 2 review
Fill in progress.md.

### Week 3

**Theme: Pyramid Principle in writing**

#### Monday — Reading (30 min)
Read Chapters 4–7 of *On Writing Well*:
- Chapter 4: Style — write in your own voice, not "professional" voice
- Chapter 7: Words — on choosing specific, concrete words over vague, abstract ones

#### Tuesday — Writing drill: Pyramid Principle (45 min)
**The drill:**
1. Pick a situation where you need to convince someone of something — a decision, a recommendation, a proposal (real or imagined)
2. Write your conclusion in one sentence. This is the top of the pyramid. Example: "We should switch to PostgreSQL."
3. List 3 reasons your conclusion is true. Each reason is a complete statement, not a topic. Example: not "Performance" but "PostgreSQL handles our query load 3x faster in benchmarks."
4. Under each reason, write 1–2 pieces of evidence: data, examples, observations.
5. Now write the whole thing in paragraph form, top-down: conclusion → reason 1 + evidence → reason 2 + evidence → reason 3 + evidence.

Check: Can someone read only the first sentence and know what you recommend? If yes, the pyramid is working.

#### Wednesday — Podcast + reflection (30 min)

#### Thursday — Apply BLUF verbally (60 min preparation + 1 real conversation)
This week's practice is about speaking, not writing.

**Preparation (10 min):**
Think of one meeting or conversation you will have today or tomorrow where you need to make a point or give an update. Write out how you would normally say it. Now rewrite it with the point in the first sentence.

**The real practice:**
In the actual meeting or conversation, lead with your point. Do not say "So I wanted to talk about..." Do not say "There's something I've been thinking about..." Start with the answer. "My recommendation is X." "The issue is Y." "I think we should Z."

**After:**
Write 3 sentences about how it felt and how the listener responded.

**Theme: PREP for spoken answers**

Focus on understanding the concept, not memorizing everything. The key question to answer after reading: "What is the difference between a topic and an argument?"

#### Tuesday — Writing drill: PREP practice (45 min)
**The drill:**
Write PREP answers to 5 common questions someone might ask you about your work.

Example questions:
- "What do you think is the biggest risk in this project?"
- "Should we switch to framework X?"
- "Why did the feature take longer than estimated?"
- "What would you do differently if you started this over?"
- "What should we prioritize next quarter?"

For each: write it out using PREP — Point, Reason, Example, Point. Keep each answer under 100 words.

The goal is to wire the structure into your brain so that when asked these questions in real life, PREP comes automatically.

## Common Mistakes in Phase 1

### Mistake 1: Thinking BLUF is too blunt
Many people resist BLUF because "it feels rude to just say the answer without explaining first." This is a cultural intuition, not a fact. In professional contexts, leading with the point is almost universally appreciated. The reader can always ask for more context. They cannot un-read three paragraphs of background they did not need.

### Mistake 2: Writing long BLUF sentences
BLUF does not mean cramming everything into one giant sentence. "I wanted to share with you that based on our analysis of the current situation and the various factors we considered over the past two weeks, we believe that the best course of action would be to..." — that is a long confused sentence, not BLUF. The first sentence should be short and clear. "We recommend moving the launch to Q3." That is BLUF.

### Mistake 3: Applying Pyramid only to formal documents
The Pyramid Principle applies to Slack messages, verbal updates, and emails too. Anytime you are making a point and supporting it with reasons, you are using a pyramid — or failing to. Practice it on small things first, not just big documents.

### Mistake 4: Writing the PREP example as an abstraction
"For example, this often happens in teams" is not an example. An example is: "In March, during the payment service migration, we underestimated the testing phase by 4 days because we did not account for the staging environment setup." Specific, concrete, real.

### Mistake 5: Skipping the daily micro-habit
The daily habit — BLUF + cut 30% on every message — is more valuable than any weekly drill. It runs on real situations, with real stakes, every day. 5 minutes per day for 90 days is 7.5 hours of deliberate practice embedded in your actual work. Do not skip it.

Record yourself giving a 3-minute structured update on a real work topic. The update should:

- [ ] Contain no filler words ("basically", "kind of", "you know", "so yeah", "um")
