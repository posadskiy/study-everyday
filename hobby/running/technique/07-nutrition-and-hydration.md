# Lesson 07 — Nutrition and Hydration

> You cannot out-train poor fueling. Nutrition is not optional maintenance — it is a core performance variable.

> **Practical reference files are in [`nutrition/`](../nutrition/).** This lesson covers the theory behind the numbers.

---

## Part 1: Daily Nutrition for a Running Athlete

### Your Caloric Baseline

At 81 kg with increasing training load, your energy needs are significantly higher than a sedentary person.

**Estimated daily caloric needs:**

| Training Day | Approximate Calories |
|-------------|---------------------|
| Rest day | 2400–2600 kcal |
| Easy run (45–60 min) | 2700–3000 kcal |
| Long run (2–3 hours) | 3200–3800 kcal |
| Very long run (4+ hours) | 3800–4500 kcal |

Most amateur runners undereat on training days. This is the most common mistake and causes:
- Poor recovery (muscles cannot rebuild)
- Chronic fatigue and declining performance
- Higher injury risk (tendons and bones need protein and calories to adapt)
- Mood disruption and brain fog

**If you are consistently tired, the first question is: are you eating enough?**

---

### Macronutrient Framework

**Carbohydrates — your primary fuel**
- Trail running is predominantly aerobic, which uses a mix of fat and carbohydrate
- At higher intensities (uphills, race pace), carbohydrate becomes the dominant fuel
- Target: 5–7g per kg bodyweight on training days → 405–567g carbs/day
- Sources: oats, rice, potatoes, pasta, bread, fruit, legumes

**Protein — recovery and adaptation**
- Muscle and connective tissue repair requires protein
- Target: 1.6–2.0g per kg bodyweight/day → 130–160g protein/day
- Distribute across meals: 30–40g per meal is more effective than one large dose
- Sources: eggs, chicken, fish, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, legumes, whey protein
- Post-run priority: 30–40g protein within 30–60 minutes of finishing

**Fat — sustained energy and joint health**
- Important for long-duration aerobic metabolism (fat oxidation)
- Target: 1.0–1.5g per kg → 80–120g fat/day
- Sources: olive oil, nuts, avocado, oily fish (salmon, sardines), eggs

---

### Meal Timing for a Runner

**Pre-run (1–3 hours before):**
- Carbohydrate-rich, moderate protein, low fat and fiber (to avoid GI issues)
- Example: oats with banana and honey, rice with a small amount of protein
- If running first thing in morning: a banana or sports gel 20 min before is sufficient for runs under 75 min

**Post-run (within 30 min):**
- Protein + carbohydrates to initiate recovery
- Example: Greek yogurt with fruit, recovery shake (whey + banana), eggs on toast
- The 30-minute window matters more after hard efforts than easy runs

**Before bed:**
- Casein protein (cottage cheese, Greek yogurt) promotes overnight muscle repair
- Especially relevant if doing two-a-day sessions or high training weeks

---

### Foods to Prioritize as Volume Increases

As your weekly mileage increases, your body will signal needs through cravings. Common legitimate cravings:
- **Salty foods:** sodium lost through sweat — add salt to food, eat olives, pickles, pretzels
- **Carbohydrates:** glycogen depletion — this is real, eat the pasta
- **Red meat or eggs:** iron and protein, especially after weeks with lots of climbing
- **Citrus and fruits:** Vitamin C for immune support (high training load suppresses immunity temporarily)

**Iron note:** mountain runners doing high volume are at moderate risk for iron deficiency, particularly runners who sweat heavily. If you feel chronically fatigued and training looks correct, get a blood panel including ferritin.

## Part 2: Fueling During Runs

### When On-Run Fueling Is Required

- **Under 60–75 minutes:** water only is usually sufficient
- **60–90 minutes:** optional gel or small snack, definitely stay hydrated
- **90 min – 3 hours:** fuel every 30–45 minutes is necessary
- **3+ hours:** fuel every 30 minutes without fail; use both carbs and real food

### The Golden Rule of On-Run Fueling

**Eat before you are hungry. Drink before you are thirsty.**

Both hunger and thirst lag behind actual physiological need by 15–30 minutes. By the time you feel the signal, your performance is already declining. Set a timer. Fuel on schedule.

---

### Carbohydrate Intake Targets

| Duration | Carbs per Hour |
|---------|---------------|
| 60–90 min | 30–60g/hour |
| 90 min – 2.5 hours | 60g/hour |
| 2.5 hours+ | 60–90g/hour |

**Why 90g max?** The gut can only absorb one type of carbohydrate transporter at full speed. Above 60g/hour from glucose alone, absorption stalls. At 90g/hour, you need a **glucose + fructose combination** (different transport pathways). Most sport products account for this with mixed carbs.

Going above 90g/hour without gut training often causes GI distress (nausea, cramps, diarrhea during running). Start conservatively.

---

### Fuel Sources: Comparison

#### Energy Gels
- Fast absorbing, easy to carry, work reliably
- 21–25g carbs per gel (Maurten, SiS, Gu, Huma)
- Take with water (especially maltodextrin-based gels)
- Cons: flavor fatigue on long events; some cause GI issues
- **Recommended for beginners:** Maurten 100 (easy on stomach) or SiS GO isotonic (can take without water)

#### Chews / Blocks
- Slower to absorb than gels, which can be an advantage for steady fueling
- Require chewing — avoid on very technical terrain where mental focus is needed

#### Real Food (for runs over 3 hours)
- Dates, bananas, boiled potatoes with salt, rice balls, PB&J cut small
- Better tolerated than gels by many runners over long durations
- Requires carrying in a vest pocket
- Trial and practice needed to identify what your stomach accepts while running

#### Liquid Fuel
- Sports drinks, Tailwind, Maurten drink mix
- Combines hydration and calories
- Useful in vest soft flasks
- Risk: if you misjudge concentration, can cause GI issues

---

### Race Day Fueling Plan Template

Before building your race plan, practice fueling on training runs of similar duration. Your race-day plan should have zero surprises.

**Example plan for a 32km / 7-hour race:**

| Time | Action |
|------|--------|
| 60 min before start | Real meal: rice or oats, banana, coffee if habitual |
| 15 min before start | 1 gel or banana + electrolyte drink |
| Every 30–35 min | 1 gel (targeting 60g carbs/hour) |
| Aid stations | Eat anything easily digested: banana, broth, boiled potato |
| Hours 4–7 | Switch partially to real food if available |
| Post-finish | Recovery meal within 30 min: protein + carbs |

## Part 3: Hydration

### Daily Hydration Baseline
- Minimum: 2.5–3.5 liters of water/day for a runner
- On long training days: 3.5–5 liters
- Urine color test: pale yellow = adequate; dark yellow = dehydrated; clear = possibly over-hydrated

### During Runs

| Conditions | Intake per Hour |
|-----------|----------------|
| Cool (below 15°C) | 400–600 ml/hour |
| Warm (15–25°C) | 600–750 ml/hour |
| Hot (above 25°C) | 750–1000 ml/hour |

**Note:** over-hydration is also dangerous (hyponatremia — dangerously low sodium). Do not drink large volumes of plain water without also replacing electrolytes on runs over 2 hours.

### Electrolytes

Sweat contains sodium, potassium, magnesium, and chloride. Water alone does not replace these.

**Signs of sodium deficiency during running:**
- Swelling in hands or feet
- Nausea
- Headache
- Confusion in extreme cases (late-stage hyponatremia)

**Solutions:**
- Add 400–600mg sodium per liter of water on runs over 90 minutes
- Use electrolyte tablets (SaltStick, Precision Hydration, Nuun)
- Eat salty foods at aid stations
- Pre-hydrate with electrolytes the evening before long runs or races

### The Karst Climate Consideration

The Karst plateau is exposed and can be very hot in July–August (35°C+). The Bora wind can also cause rapid dehydration through evaporation. In summer racing or long runs:
- Start with full hydration (1.5 liters in vest for 3+ hour runs)
- Increase electrolyte intake above baseline
- Know where water sources are on your routes (springs, aid stations)
- Do long runs early morning in summer (before 8am)

## Gut Training

The ability to absorb carbohydrates while running is a trainable adaptation. Runners who practice fueling on long training runs gradually develop higher tolerance.

**Start low:** begin with 30g carbs/hour and increase by 5–10g/hour over months.  
**Practice in training:** do not eat gels for the first time in a race. Try everything in training.  
**Common triggers for running GI issues:** high fiber before runs, dehydration, too-concentrated carb solution, NSAID painkillers (avoid ibuprofen during racing).

## Key Takeaways

1. Eat more than you think. Undereating is the most common amateur error.
2. 30–40g protein post-run within 30–60 minutes.
3. Fuel every 30–45 minutes on runs over 90 minutes. On schedule, not when hungry.
4. Target 60–90g carbs per hour for events over 2.5 hours.
5. Practice your race nutrition in training. Zero surprises on race day.
6. Add electrolytes to water on any run over 90 minutes.
