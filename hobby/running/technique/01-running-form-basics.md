# Lesson 01 — Running Form Basics

> Good form is not about looking like an elite. It is about moving efficiently with the least damage to your body over years.

---

## Why Form Matters for Mountain Running

On flat road, sloppy form costs you a little efficiency. On mountain trails, sloppy form causes:
- Ankle rolls on rocks
- Knee pain on descents (the most common reason people quit trail running)
- Early quad failure in races
- Back pain on long days

You will not fix form instantly. The goal is to build the right movement patterns over 3–6 months of consistent practice. Awareness comes first.

---

## The Core Elements

### 1. Posture — the Stack

Think of your body as a vertical stack: ankles, hips, shoulders, head all roughly aligned.

**Common mistake:** bending forward at the waist. This compresses the lower back and shifts your center of mass incorrectly.

**Correct:** lean forward slightly from the **ankles**, not the waist. Your whole body tilts as one unit, like a falling tree. This is subtle — 2–5 degrees — but critically different from waist-hinge.

On uphills: increase the lean naturally as gradient increases. Your body should feel like it is falling into the hill.

### 2. Head and Eyes

- Look **2–4 meters ahead** on trail, not at your feet.
- On technical terrain with rocks/roots, briefly scan 1 meter ahead to place your next 2–3 steps, then return gaze forward.
- Avoid constant downward stare — it causes neck tension and kills your balance feedback loop.
- Your peripheral vision handles immediate foot placement; your conscious gaze handles terrain reading.

On downhills: extend your gaze further — 5–10 meters — to choose your line. Terrain reading is a skill that develops with experience.

### 3. Arm Swing

Arms are not decoration. They:
- Counterbalance the rotation of your legs
- Drive momentum on uphills
- Act as a stabilizer on technical terrain

**Correct arm swing:**
- Elbows bent at roughly 90 degrees
- Swing forward-to-back, not across the body
- Hands relaxed — imagine holding a potato chip without crushing it
- On uphills: pump arms more aggressively, they add power
- On technical terrain: arms out slightly wider for balance (like a tightrope walker)

### 4. Foot Strike — Midfoot on Trail

Road runners debate heel vs forefoot strike. On trail, the answer is more pragmatic:

**Target: midfoot to forefoot strike**, landing under your center of mass (not in front of you).

- Heel striking is a braking force — it slows you down and increases joint impact
- Full forefoot (tiptoe) is exhausting on long days — reserve for short climbs
- **Midfoot** landing is sustainable: good energy return, manageable calf load

On uphills: naturally shift toward forefoot as gradient increases.
On downhills: foot placement and quick contact time matter more than strike position — see Lesson 03.

### 5. Cadence — Step Rate

**Target: 170–180 steps per minute** on runnable terrain.

Why it matters: lower cadence = longer strides = foot landing in front of body = braking force + higher impact. Higher cadence = shorter strides = landing under body = less impact.

On trails, cadence naturally varies with terrain. Do not obsess about hitting exactly 180. The cue is: **take shorter, quicker steps rather than longer, slower ones.**

To practice: count your steps for 20 seconds and multiply by 3. If you are at 150, gradually work toward 160, then 170 over months. Do not force it overnight.

Free metronome apps can help with cadence training during easy runs.

### 6. Stride Length — Adapt to Terrain

Mountain running requires constantly adjusting stride length. There is no single "correct" length.

- **Flat runnable trail:** moderate stride, natural
- **Technical rocky terrain:** shorten stride significantly. Short quick steps give more placement options and better balance
- **Uphills:** shorten stride, increase cadence
- **Downhills:** read Lesson 03 before thinking about this

The key insight: **on trail, stride shortening is almost always the right adaptation.** Most beginners try to maintain road stride length and this causes problems.

### 7. Hip Drive and Glute Engagement

Your glutes (gluteus maximus, medius) should be the primary power source, not your quads. Quad-dominant running causes early fatigue and knee problems.

To engage glutes: think about pushing the ground **behind** you, not just picking your feet up. The "push-back" cue activates the posterior chain.

This is hard to feel at first. The strength work in Lesson 06 will help build this pattern.

## Common Form Errors to Watch For

| Error | Symptom | Fix |
|-------|---------|-----|
| Waist hinge (bent at hips) | Lower back pain, early fatigue | Lean from ankles, stack the body |
| Overstriding | Shin splints, knee pain | Shorter steps, land under body |
| Looking at feet constantly | Tripping, neck tension | Eyes 2–4m ahead, trust periphery |
| Arms crossing body | Wasted rotation energy | Swing forward-back, not across |
| Heel striking on downhills | Knee pain, braking | See Lesson 03 in detail |
| Rigid upper body | Poor balance on technical | Relax shoulders, soft hands |

## A Practical Drill for Your Next Run

**10-minute form check at the start of every easy run:**

1. First 2 min: focus on posture. Stack the body, ankle lean.
2. Next 2 min: focus on arms. Forward-back swing, relaxed hands.
3. Next 2 min: focus on eyes. Look ahead, not down.
4. Next 2 min: focus on foot strike. Land under body, midfoot.
5. Last 2 min: put it all together, then forget about it and run naturally.

Over months, these movements become automatic.

## Video Resources

**Watch these:**
- **Sage Running** on YouTube: search "running form trail running" — Nate Helming's form videos are concise and technical
- **iRunFar**: search "trail running technique" — practical guides with terrain context
- Any slow-motion footage of Kilian Jornet or Jim Walmsley on flat trail — observe ankle lean, arm swing, foot placement. Do not worry about the pace.

**What to look for when watching elites:**
- How short their stride is on technical terrain
- How far ahead they look
- How relaxed their upper body is even at hard effort
- How quickly their feet contact and leave the ground (quick, light, not stomping)
