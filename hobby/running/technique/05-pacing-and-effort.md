# Lesson 05 — Pacing and Effort

> GPS pace is a lie on mountain terrain. Learn to pace by feel, and your race performance will improve immediately.

---

## Why GPS Pace Does Not Work on Trails

On flat road, a 6:00/km means the same thing every time. On a trail with elevation change, the same energy expenditure might produce 5:00/km on the descent and 12:00/km on the climb. Your GPS watch will show wildly different numbers on the same effort.

Running by GPS pace on trails causes:
- Going too hard on uphills (chasing a pace target)
- Going too easy on downhills (not using the recovery)
- Burning out in the first third of a race
- Confusion and frustration

**The tools that work on mountain terrain:**
1. Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE)
2. Heart rate
3. The talk test
4. Experience

---

## Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE)

The RPE scale (1–10) is your primary pacing tool. It is the subjective sense of how hard you are working, accounting for everything: elevation, heat, fatigue, hydration.

| RPE | How It Feels | Breathing | Pace Context |
|-----|-------------|-----------|--------------|
| 1–2 | Almost nothing | Normal conversation | Walking flat |
| 3–4 | Comfortable, easy | Full sentences | Easy trail jog |
| 5–6 | Moderate, aware of effort | Short sentences | Aerobic running |
| 7–8 | Hard, focused | Few words | Threshold pace |
| 9 | Very hard, dig deep | Cannot speak | Max climb |
| 10 | All-out sprint | Gasping | Short intervals |

**For training:**
- Easy days: RPE 3–4
- Aerobic long runs: RPE 4–5
- Tempo/threshold: RPE 7
- Hill repeats: RPE 8–9 on climb, RPE 3 on descent

**For a 30km+ race:**
- First quarter: RPE 4–5 (feels too easy — this is correct)
- Middle half: RPE 5–6
- Final quarter: RPE 6–7 if you paced well

## Heart Rate as a Secondary Tool

Heart rate is more objective than RPE but has limitations on trails (lag time, heat effect, caffeine, altitude). Use it alongside RPE.

### Finding Your Zones

The simplest field estimate of max heart rate for training purposes:
- For mountain running: 220 minus age = rough max (for you: ~188 bpm)
- This formula is unreliable for individuals — actual max is best found in a time trial

| Zone | % of Max HR | RPE | Purpose |
|------|-------------|-----|---------|
| Z1 Easy | 60–70% | 3–4 | Recovery, warm-up |
| Z2 Aerobic | 70–80% | 4–5 | Base building (most training) |
| Z3 Tempo | 80–87% | 6–7 | Threshold |
| Z4 Hard | 87–93% | 8 | Intervals, hard climbs |
| Z5 Max | 93–100% | 9–10 | Short sprints, max efforts |

**For you at 32 with an estimated max of ~188:**
- Z1: below ~132 bpm
- Z2: 132–150 bpm
- Z3: 150–163 bpm
- Z4: 163–175 bpm
- Z5: above 175 bpm

These are starting estimates. After a few months with a GPS watch, you will calibrate better from actual data.

### HR on Uphills
Heart rate rises on uphills even when pace drops. You may be at Z3 heart rate while barely moving. This is normal. Use heart rate as the primary limiter: if HR is above your Z3 ceiling, slow down or hike regardless of how fast or slow you are moving.

### Cardiac Drift
On long runs (2+ hours), heart rate gradually rises for the same effort due to fluid loss, heat, and fatigue. Expect Z2 to feel harder after 2 hours than at the start. This is normal.

## Effort Zones and Training Distribution

**The 80/20 principle** (from training science, not intuition):

80% of your training volume should be easy (Z1–Z2).  
20% can be moderate to hard (Z3–Z5).

Most beginners do the opposite — they run moderately hard on every run, never easy enough to build aerobic base, never hard enough to get true training stimulus. This is called "grey zone" training and is the least effective approach.

**Practical implication:**
- If you run 5 times per week, 4 of those runs should be easy. Genuinely easy.
- One session can be hard (hill repeats, intervals, tempo).
- Long run: primarily Z1–Z2, with Z3 on hard terrain sections.

## Pacing a 30km+ Race

This is where pacing knowledge has the most immediate payoff. The single biggest mistake in mountain trail races: **going out too fast.**

### The Ideal Pacing Shape

```
Effort (RPE)
   8 |                                             /
   7 |                                          /
   6 |                             ___________/
   5 |           _________________/
   4 | __________/
   3 |
       Start   25%   50%   75%  100% (Finish)
```

Effort increases gradually. You feel too easy at the start. This is correct.

### Race Pacing Rules

**Rule 1: Walk more than you think you need to in the first half.**  
Every minute of walking early saves 5 minutes of suffering late. This is the veteran's trade secret.

**Rule 2: The race starts at 60% of the distance.**  
For a 32km race, the actual race starts at km 19. Everything before that is just getting to the race.

**Rule 3: Eat and drink before you are hungry or thirsty.**  
Hunger and thirst lag behind actual need. By the time you feel them, you are already behind.

**Rule 4: Never make a big surge in the first half.**  
If someone passes you, let them go. If you feel great at km 10, do not speed up.

**Rule 5: Positive split acceptance.**  
It is normal for the second half to be slower than the first. Plan for this. A very small positive split (5–10% slower in second half) means good pacing.

### For Your Next 32km Race (Specific Numbers)

Based on your 7.5h finish:
- Target first half: ~4 hours (slightly slower than average)
- Target second half: ~3.5 hours
- This gives a slight negative split improvement vs. your first finish

If you aim for 6.5h total:
- First 16km: ~3h 15min
- Second 16km: ~3h 15min
- End result: more energy in the final sections, stronger finish

## Grade Adjusted Pace (GAP)

Modern GPS watches (Garmin, Coros) offer Grade Adjusted Pace — a pace calculation that accounts for elevation change to show "equivalent flat pace." This is more useful than raw pace on trails.

Once you have a watch, use GAP to calibrate effort on varied terrain.

---

## Perceived Effort Calibration: A Practical Test

**Every 2–3 months, do this:**

1. Go to a flat runnable trail section (500m+ with no major grade change)
2. Run 10 minutes at what feels like RPE 5 (moderate aerobic)
3. Record your pace from your GPS watch
4. Note your heart rate

Over time, as fitness improves, the pace at RPE 5 will increase while heart rate stays the same. This is aerobic improvement made visible.

## Key Takeaways

1. Ignore GPS pace on trails. Use RPE and heart rate.
2. 80% easy, 20% hard — most training should feel almost too easy.
3. In races: start conservatively, increase effort in the final third.
4. Fueling and pacing are the same problem: both require acting before you feel the need.
5. Build a database of effort-to-performance calibration data over months and years.
