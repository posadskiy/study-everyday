# Lesson 04 — Breathing and Cadence

> Your breathing is the most immediate feedback system you have. Learn to read it before you buy any gadget.

---

## Why Breathing Matters More Than Pace

On a mountain trail, GPS pace is nearly meaningless. A "7:00/km" can be flat runnable path or a 30% rocky climb — effort is completely different. Your breathing rate, however, reflects your actual physiological effort regardless of terrain.

Learning to calibrate your effort through breathing is the single cheapest and most reliable pacing tool you have.

---

## The Three Breathing Zones

### Zone 1 — Conversational (Easy)
- You can speak full sentences comfortably
- Could maintain this pace for hours
- Nasal breathing possible
- This is where 70–80% of your training should happen
- Feels almost too easy at first. That is correct.

### Zone 2 — Aerobic Base (Moderate)
- You can speak in short sentences of 4–5 words
- Breathing is noticeable but rhythmic and controlled
- Could sustain for 1–2 hours at a stretch
- This is the optimal training zone for building aerobic capacity
- Most beginners run too fast and push out of this zone

### Zone 3 — Threshold (Hard)
- You can say only a few words at a time
- Breathing is deliberate and effortful
- Sustainable for 20–45 minutes at most
- Use sparingly in training — not every day
- Race effort for short events (VK, short trail races)

### Zone 4/5 — VO2 Max / Redline
- Cannot speak
- Breathing is gasping, ragged
- Sustainable for 3–8 minutes maximum
- For intervals and short hill repeats only
- **Never your long run pace.** Never.

**Most common beginner mistake:** spending long runs in Zone 3 or pushing into Zone 4. This feels productive but causes fatigue accumulation without proportional fitness gain. The aerobic base is built in Zones 1–2.

---

## Nasal vs. Mouth Breathing

**At low intensity (Z1):** try to breathe through your nose. Nasal breathing:
- Filters and humidifies air
- Slightly restricts airflow, training your body to be efficient at lower oxygen delivery
- Some evidence for better CO2 tolerance and more efficient oxygen extraction
- Signals your brain to stay calm

**At moderate intensity (Z2):** transition to in through nose, out through mouth, or both mouth.

**At high intensity (Z3+):** full mouth breathing. Do not restrict yourself. You need maximum airflow.

Do not force nasal breathing during hard efforts — it serves no purpose beyond Z2 and impairs performance.

### How to learn belly (diaphragmatic) breathing

Most people default to chest breathing — shallow, only the top third of the lungs. Belly breathing uses the full lung volume.

**Learn it lying down first:**
1. Lie on your back, one hand on chest, one hand on belly
2. Inhale through your nose — only the belly hand should rise; chest hand stays still
3. Exhale — belly falls
4. If your chest rises first, you are chest breathing

Once you feel it lying down, practice sitting → standing → easy walking → Zone 1 running. It becomes automatic over weeks.

**While running:** on inhale, belly pushes outward (diaphragm drops, lungs fill from the bottom). Chest stays quiet. Quick check: if your shoulders rise with each breath, you are chest breathing — drop the shoulders and redirect the breath lower.

**When to practice it:** Zone 1–2 easy runs only. At Zone 3+ your body takes over naturally with faster breathing — do not try to control it there.

## Rhythmic Breathing Patterns

Rhythmic breathing means synchronizing your breath with your footfalls. This:
- Creates a regular breathing pattern that is more efficient than irregular gasping
- Helps you find and maintain a consistent pace
- Distributes the impact stress of landing across both sides of the body

**Common patterns:**

| Pattern | Inhale steps | Exhale steps | Best for |
|---------|-------------|-------------|----------|
| 3:2 | 3 | 2 | Easy effort, flat trail |
| 2:2 | 2 | 2 | Moderate effort |
| 2:1 | 2 | 1 | Hard effort, uphills |

These are reference points, not rules. On technical terrain your breathing will be erratic — do not try to force patterns during scrambling sections.

## Breathing on Uphills

Uphills always demand more oxygen. Expect your breathing to increase. Strategies:

- **Accept the increase:** do not try to suppress it. Your body is working correctly.
- **Belly breathing:** breathe from your diaphragm, not your chest. Belly should visibly expand on inhale. Chest breathing is shallow and inefficient.
- **Exhale fully:** many runners fail to fully exhale, accumulating stale air. Forced complete exhale improves next inhale quality.
- **The talk test:** if you cannot say "I am running uphill" without gasping, slow down or switch to power hiking. This is Zone 3+ territory.
- **Altitude:** above 2000m, you will need to breathe more at the same pace. This affects training in the Alps but less so at typical Karst elevations.

### Easy days with climbs: hike the uphill

On a recovery or easy run day (Wed/Thu), the zone is the target — not the terrain. If your route has 200m D+ and the uphill pushes you into Zone 3, **walk it**. Brisk power hiking keeps you in Zone 1–2 and still trains the uphill-specific muscles. Running the uphill "just to run it" turns a recovery day into a moderate day — you get neither the recovery benefit nor a real training stimulus.

Expect to hike 30–50% of an easy-day trail run with significant elevation. This is correct and normal at all levels, including elite.

## Cadence in Depth

### What Is Cadence?
Steps per minute (spm). Each foot contact counts as one step.

### Target Range
170–180 spm on runnable terrain is the commonly cited target. More nuanced reality:

- **Below 160 spm:** usually indicates overstriding (landing too far ahead)
- **160–170 spm:** acceptable, especially for taller runners
- **170–180 spm:** efficient range for most runners
- **180+ spm:** appropriate for technical terrain where short quick steps prevent ankle rolls

On uphills, cadence naturally drops slightly. On downhills, increase cadence above your flat baseline.

### Why Cadence Matters Mechanically
Higher cadence with shorter steps means:
- Foot lands closer to under your center of mass
- Less braking force at each landing
- Lower peak impact force
- Better proprioceptive feedback (more frequent ground contact = better terrain sensing)

### Measuring Your Cadence
Most GPS watches measure cadence automatically. Alternatively:
1. Count every time your right foot strikes the ground for 30 seconds
2. Multiply by 4 (= total steps per minute)

### Increasing Cadence Gradually
If your natural cadence is 155, do not jump to 180 immediately. Increase by 5 spm per month. Rapid increases cause calf and Achilles strain because shorter steps shift load forward.

**Cadence drill:** during a 20-minute easy run, use a free metronome app (search "running cadence metronome" on your phone) at 170 bpm for 5 minutes. Note how different it feels from your natural rhythm.

## Breathing and Cadence at Altitude

If you run at high altitude (above 2000–2500m in the Alps), expect:
- Breathing rate increases significantly at the same pace
- Heart rate may be higher than expected
- Performance is reduced 1–2% per 300m above 1500m
- Adaptation happens in days to weeks (acute acclimatization)
- For day trips above 2000m from Sežana: allow extra time, go slower, stay very well hydrated

For local Karst and Nanos training (900–1300m): minimal altitude effect. For Triglav or high alpine events (2000m+): plan for slower paces.

## Practical Application: The Cadence + Breathing Check

**Before each run, decide your target zone:**
- Easy recovery run: Zone 1 (conversational, nasal if possible)
- Aerobic base run: Zone 2 (short sentences)
- Hill repeats: Zone 3 climbing, Zone 1 descent

**During the run:**
- Check in with your breathing every 10–15 minutes
- If you cannot speak comfortably, slow down
- If you feel like you could go much faster, push slightly

**After the run:**
- Was the effort appropriate for the session type?
- Did your breathing feel controlled or ragged?
- How quickly did your breathing recover after hard sections?

Recovery speed is a fitness marker. A fitter runner returns to conversational breathing in 30–60 seconds after a hard climb. A beginner may take 2–3 minutes. This improves rapidly.

## Key Takeaways

1. Breathing rate is your best free pacing tool on variable terrain.
2. 70–80% of your runs should be at easy/conversational pace — this is not wasted time, it is building your engine.
3. Breathe from your belly (diaphragm), not your chest.
4. Target 170–180 cadence on runnable trail — build to this gradually.
5. Cadence and breathing are connected: faster cadence naturally regulates effort.
