# Module 6 — The Art of Tasting

Tasting wine is a skill. Like any skill, it requires practice and a framework. The framework is not complicated — it is a structured way of paying attention to what is already in front of you.

The goal is not to identify every aromatic molecule or hit a correct answer. The goal is to observe, describe, and ultimately understand what makes one wine different from another.

---

## Before You Begin

**Glassware matters.** A proper wine glass — tulip-shaped, reasonably large — concentrates aromas at the rim. A tumbler or a very small glass makes tasting harder. You don't need expensive crystal, but shape matters.

**Temperature matters.** Red wine at room temperature if the room is 25°C is too warm — you smell alcohol instead of fruit. Whites served at 5°C are too cold — aromas are suppressed. See Module 9 for serving temperatures.

**Context matters.** Strong perfume, cooking smells, and scented candles interfere with wine aromas. Professional tastings are done in neutral environments for this reason.

**Pour the right amount.** About one-third of a glass for tasting — enough to swirl without spilling.

---

## The 5 S's Framework

A systematic approach used by professionals and enthusiasts alike:

```
See → Swirl → Sniff → Sip → Savor
```

Work through these in order, every time. With practice, the whole process takes 2–3 minutes and becomes automatic.

---

## 1. See

Hold the glass against a white background (a napkin, a piece of paper). Observe:

### Color

**In whites:**
- Pale yellow / almost colorless → Cool climate, young, unoaked (Mosel Riesling, Muscadet, young Pinot Grigio)
- Straw / light gold → Typical young white wine
- Deep gold / amber → Oaked, older, botrytis-affected, or skin-contact (orange wine)
- Brown tinge → Very old, or oxidized (not necessarily a fault — Madeira and Fino Sherry are intentionally oxidized)

**In reds:**
- Purple / violet → Young wine (Malbec, young Syrah)
- Ruby → Typical young-to-mid age red
- Garnet → Medium age, some maturity
- Brick / orange rim → Older wine, some development (the rim fades before the center)
- Tawny → Aged wines, oxidation (Tawny Port, old Grenache)

**In rosé:**
- Pale salmon / copper → Dry Provence style
- Deep pink / fuchsia → More fruit-forward, possibly slightly sweet

### Intensity

Pale vs. deep color:
- **Deep color** in reds often indicates riper fruit, hotter climate, more extraction, more tannin potential
- **Pale color** does not mean lesser wine — Pinot Noir is inherently pale, and Burgundy Grand Cru is among the world's finest

### Clarity

- **Clear and bright:** Well-made and stable
- **Cloudy or hazy:** Can be deliberate (unfiltered natural wine, Pét-Nat) or a fault (bacterial spoilage)
- **Particles/sediment:** Normal in older reds (tartrate crystals in whites are also harmless)

### Legs / Tears

The streaks that run down the inside of the glass after swirling. Caused by the interaction of alcohol and glycerol with surface tension. **Thick, slow-moving legs indicate higher alcohol and/or glycerol (body).** They are *not* a quality indicator — just an observation.

---

## 2. Swirl

Swirl the wine in the glass — either on the table (easier and less spillage risk) or in the air. This:
- Aerates the wine, releasing volatile aromatic compounds
- Warms the wine very slightly
- Lets you observe the legs

Swirl for 3–5 seconds before smelling.

---

## 3. Sniff

This is where the complexity lives. The sense of smell is directly connected to memory and emotion in ways taste alone is not.

**Technique:**
1. Before swirling: a quick sniff to get the first impression
2. After swirling: a longer, deeper sniff with your nose inside the glass
3. Try one nostril at a time — many tasters find they detect different things
4. Move in and out — hover at different distances from the surface

**Don't overthink it.** Trust your first instincts. If it smells like cherries, it smells like cherries. Overthinking produces vague non-answers.

### Why Smell Is the Most Important Sense for Wine

Aroma molecules are volatile — they evaporate from the wine's surface and bind to olfactory receptors in the nose. What we call "flavor" is almost entirely retronasal smell: aromatic molecules travel from the back of the mouth up through the nasal passage while drinking. The tongue itself can only detect five things — sweet, sour, bitter, salty, and umami. Everything else that you think of as "taste" — the cherry, the pepper, the mineral note — is actually smell.

This is why a blocked nose turns wine into a featureless, slightly acidic, slightly alcoholic liquid. It also explains why sniffing carefully is not just ritual — it is the majority of the information.

### Primary Aromas

These come from the grape itself — the variety's natural compounds.

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| Fresh fruit | Apple, pear, peach, citrus, cherry, raspberry, blackcurrant |
| Tropical fruit | Mango, passion fruit, pineapple, guava (warm climate wines) |
| Dried fruit | Raisin, prune, fig, dried apricot |
| Floral | Rose, violet, jasmine, orange blossom, elderflower |
| Herbal | Grass, herbs, thyme, bay leaf, mint, eucalyptus |
| Vegetal | Green pepper, asparagus, tomato leaf |
| Mineral | Slate, flint, chalk, wet stone, saline |

### Secondary Aromas

These arise from fermentation — produced by yeast activity. Some of these compounds barely exist in the grape at all; they are created by yeast metabolism during fermentation.

| Character | Compound (if known) | Associated With |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------|
| Bread, brioche, biscuit | Yeast autolysis products | Lees aging — aged sparkling wine especially |
| Butter, cream | Diacetyl | Malolactic fermentation in white wines |
| Cheese, yogurt | Various MLF products | MLF in some white wines |
| Banana, bubblegum, pear candy | Isoamyl acetate (ester) | Carbonic maceration (Beaujolais), some Prosecco |
| Tropical fruit (passionfruit, guava) | Thiols | Yeast-released from grape precursors — Sauvignon Blanc especially |
| Black pepper | Rotundone | Found in grape, amplified by fermentation — Syrah signature |
| Slightly funky, earthy | Multiple | Wild yeast fermentation |

**A worked example — Sauvignon Blanc aromatic families:** The same grape can produce dramatically different aromatic profiles depending on climate, ripeness, and yeast. The families and their sources:

| Family | Aroma | Chemical Driver |
|--------|-------|-----------------|
| Green / herbal | Grass, green pepper, nettle, tomato leaf | Methoxypyrazines (from the grape — more pronounced in cool climates and underripe fruit) |
| Citrus | Lime, grapefruit, lemon zest | Terpenes and acids |
| Tropical | Passionfruit, guava, mango | Thiols (released from odourless precursors in the grape by yeast enzymes) |
| Floral | Elderflower, jasmine | Esters and terpenes |
| Mineral | Flint, wet stone, chalk | Origin debated — may relate to sulfur compounds or soil influence on yeast metabolism |

A Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc is thiol-dominant (tropical). A Sancerre is methoxypyrazine-dominant (mineral and green). Both are the same grape — the difference is climate, ripeness level, and yeast.

### Tertiary Aromas (Bouquet)

These develop during aging — in barrel or in bottle. The wine is no longer purely "fruity."

| Character | Source |
|-----------|--------|
| Vanilla, toast, coconut, smoke | Oak barrel aging |
| Cedar, cigar box, pencil shaving | Oak + bottle age (Cabernet) |
| Leather, tobacco, earth | Bottle age in reds |
| Mushroom, forest floor, truffle | Bottle age in Pinot Noir |
| Petrol, kerosene | Bottle age in Riesling (the famous TDN compound) |
| Honey, wax, dried flowers | Aged whites, botrytis |
| Coffee, chocolate | Oak + age in full-bodied reds |

### The Aroma Wheel

The UC Davis Wine Aroma Wheel (Ann Noble, 1984) organizes wine aromas into a systematic visual reference. The categories:

```
Fruit → Vegetative → Nutty → Caramelized → Woody → 
Earthy → Chemical → Pungent → Oxidized → Microbiological → Floral → Spicy
```

You don't need to memorize the whole wheel. Know the major categories and where your nose falls within them.

## 4. Sip

Take a medium-sized sip (not a tiny taste, not a large gulp). Let the wine coat your entire mouth — front, sides, back, roof, and tongue. Breathe in slightly through your teeth to volatilize more aromatic compounds (this technique is called "chewing" or *retro-nasal* analysis).

Evaluate these components:

### Sweetness

Detected at the tip of the tongue. Most wine is dry (residual sugar < 4 g/L). Off-dry, medium, and sweet wines have increasing residual sugar.

**Common misconception:** Fruity ≠ sweet. A wine can be very fruit-forward and smell sweet but be completely dry.

### Acidity

The mouthwatering sensation, detected at the sides of the tongue and under the jaw. High acidity makes saliva gland work — your mouth waters.

- **High acidity wines:** Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Chablis, Champagne, Pinot Noir, Barolo, Barbera
- **Lower acidity wines:** Viognier, Gewürztraminer, Grenache, Malbec

Acidity is a preservative and a structural element in aging. Wines without it can taste flat and fat.

### Tannin

Tannins are polyphenols found in grape skins, seeds, and stems (and extracted from oak). They create a drying, gripping sensation in the mouth — you can feel your gums drying out.

- **High tannin:** Cabernet Sauvignon, Barolo (Nebbiolo), Brunello, Syrah (in cool climate), Schioppettino, Pignolo, Teran
- **Low tannin:** Pinot Noir, Gamay, Grenache
- **Zero tannin (almost):** All white and rosé wines

Tannin softens with aging. Harsh young tannin becomes silky with 10+ years in bottle.

### Alcohol

Felt as warmth, particularly at the back of the throat. High-alcohol wines (14%+) can taste "hot" or burning if the fruit and acidity don't balance it.

- **Low alcohol** (8–11%): Mosel Riesling, Moscato d'Asti, many Champagnes
- **Medium alcohol** (12–13%): Most European table wines
- **High alcohol** (14–15%+): Napa Cabernet, Barossa Shiraz, Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Amarone

### Umami

The fifth basic taste — savory, brothy depth. Usually associated with food (mushrooms, aged cheese, cured meat, miso), but it appears in wine too. Sources: amino acids and glutamates released during yeast autolysis (the breakdown of dead yeast cells during extended lees contact), as well as compounds from aged grape skins.

Where to notice it: Champagne and traditional-method sparkling wines aged long on the lees; old wines (particularly aged white Burgundy and aged Barolo); wines fermented on fine lees with regular stirring (*bâtonnage*). The character is a savory, nutty, almost broth-like depth beneath the fruit — not salty, not bitter, but a satisfying, mouth-coating weight that makes you want another sip.

### Body

The weight and fullness of the wine in the mouth. Largely driven by alcohol, glycerol, and residual sugar.

- **Light body:** Under 12.5% alcohol, high acidity, no residual sugar (Riesling, Pinot Grigio, Chablis)
- **Medium body:** 12.5–13.5%
- **Full body:** Over 13.5%, low acidity, possible residual sugar (Barossa Shiraz, California Chardonnay, Amarone)

---

## 5. Savor

After swallowing (or spitting, in professional tastings), pay attention to:

### Finish

The length of time flavors persist after the wine leaves your mouth. Count in "caudalies" (seconds) if you want to be precise.

- **Short finish** (under 5 seconds): Simple wine
- **Medium finish** (5–15 seconds): Good quality
- **Long finish** (15–30 seconds): Fine wine
- **Very long finish** (30+ seconds): Great wine

A long, complex finish that evolves and changes is one of the most reliable indicators of quality.

### Complexity

How many different aroma and flavor notes can you detect? Simple wine has 1–2. Complex wine has layers that shift and evolve as the wine warms and as you continue drinking.

### Balance

Does everything work together? A wine where acidity, tannin, alcohol, fruit, and (if applicable) sweetness are in harmony is "balanced." A wine where one element dominates and creates discomfort is "unbalanced."

Great wine = great balance + great complexity + great length.

## Writing a Tasting Note

A tasting note doesn't need to be long. A professional format:

```
[Color and intensity]. On the nose, [primary aromas], with [secondary/tertiary].
On the palate, [body], [acidity], [tannin if red], [sweetness if noticeable].
[Specific flavors]. [Finish length and character].
[Overall assessment / stage of development].
```

**Example (a Collio Ribolla Gialla, conventional style):**
> Pale golden, clear. Fresh and precise on the nose — citrus blossom, green apple, a note of chalk and white peach. On the palate, medium body, high acidity, completely dry. Citrus and orchard fruit, with that characteristic bitter almond on the finish. Medium-long finish. Drink now through 2026.

**Example (an aged Barolo):**
> Deep garnet with a brick-orange rim. Complex bouquet of dried rose, tar, tobacco, leather, and under-growth. Full body, high tannin (still present but integrated), high acidity. Dark cherry, dried herbs, earthy notes. Very long finish with lingering spice. Drinking beautifully now; will evolve for another decade.

## Common Wine Faults (and How to Identify Them)

| Fault | What You Notice | Cause |
|-------|----------------|-------|
| Corked (TCA) | Wet cardboard, damp cellar, mustiness — masks all fruit | Contaminated cork (2,4,6-trichloroanisole) |
| Oxidized | Flat, brown, nutty (beyond intent), Sherry-like (not pleasantly) | Too much oxygen exposure |
| Volatile acidity (VA) | Vinegar, nail polish remover, sharp edge | Acetic acid and ethyl acetate — some VA normal, high levels are a fault |
| Reduction | Struck match, rubber, garlic — often dissipates with aeration | Sulfur compounds from reductive winemaking |
| Brett (Brettanomyces) | Barnyard, leather, band-aid, horse | Wild yeast contamination — controversial: some find low levels complex |
| Re-fermentation | Unexpected bubbles in a still wine | Residual sugar + yeast in bottle |
| Heat damage | Cooked, jammy, flat, dried fruit — cork can be pushed out | Wine overheated during transport or storage |

When a wine is corked or oxidized, it is acceptable (and expected in a restaurant) to send it back.

## The 20-Point Scale

Used by many European wine competitions and critics (including Decanter, Jancis Robinson):

| Score | Interpretation |
|-------|---------------|
| 20/20 | Truly exceptional — near-perfect |
| 18–19 | Outstanding, complex, complete |
| 16–17 | Good to very good, recommended |
| 14–15 | Average to above average |
| 12–13 | Below average |
| Under 12 | Faulty or very poor |

## Self-Quiz

1. What does "tannin" feel like in the mouth, and which grape varieties are highest in tannin?
2. What are the three categories of wine aromas and what distinguishes them?
3. How do you detect sweetness vs. fruitiness in a wine?
4. What is the most immediate indicator that a wine is "corked"?
5. What is a "long finish" and why does it indicate quality?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Tannin creates a drying, gripping, astringent sensation — your gums and the inside of your cheeks feel parched. Highest tannin: Nebbiolo (Barolo), Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah (cool climate), Schioppettino, Pignolo. Lowest: Pinot Noir, Grenache, Gamay.

2. Primary (from the grape itself — fresh fruit, flowers, herbs, minerals). Secondary (from fermentation — bread, butter, banana, yeast). Tertiary / bouquet (from aging in oak and bottle — vanilla, leather, tobacco, earth, petrol in Riesling, mushroom in aged Burgundy).

3. Sweetness is a physical sensation at the tip of the tongue and a coating, soft quality — it comes from actual residual sugar. Fruitiness is an aroma: it's what you smell. A wine can smell intensely of ripe fruit and still be completely dry. Test: hold your nose and sip. If you still feel something sweet and soft, there is residual sugar. If the sensation disappears, it's just aroma.

4. A damp cardboard, wet basement, or musty cloth smell that masks all the fruit. Caused by TCA (trichloroanisole) from a contaminated cork. There is no "fix" — the bottle is faulty and should be returned in a restaurant or put aside if you bought it retail.

5. The finish is how long flavors persist after you swallow. A long finish (15–30+ seconds) means the wine has concentration, complexity, and well-integrated components that continue to evolve on the palate. Simple or dilute wines have short finishes because there isn't enough substance to sustain them.

</details>

## Practical Exercise

**Structured tasting exercise:** Open a bottle of wine you haven't had before. Work through every element of the 5 S's systematically and write a full tasting note using the template above. Don't worry about being "right" — the goal is to pay attention and put observations into words.

Do this for 10 different wines over the next month. Your descriptive vocabulary will expand significantly, and you'll start noticing differences between wines that previously all seemed "similar."
