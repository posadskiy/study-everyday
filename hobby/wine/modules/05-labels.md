# Module 5 — Reading Wine Labels

A wine label is a legal document, a marketing tool, and a source of genuine information — all at once. Learning to decode it quickly is one of the most practical wine skills. This module covers how labels work in each major wine-producing country.

---

## What Every Wine Label Must Show (by law, EU)

1. **Category of wine** (wine, sparkling wine, liqueur wine, etc.)
2. **Volume** (750 ml, 375 ml, 1.5L, etc.)
3. **Alcohol content** (% vol)
4. **Producer/bottler name and location**
5. **Country of origin**
6. **Lot number** (a small code, usually meaningless for consumers)
7. **Contains sulfites** (if > 10 mg/L — almost all wine exceeds this)
8. **Allergen information** (since 2023: ingredients and nutrition facts on EU wines)

Everything else — grape variety, vintage, appellation, quality tier, tasting notes — is optional but frequently included.

## France

French labels are the most confusing for newcomers because they **lead with place, not grape**. "Chablis" means nothing unless you know it's 100% Chardonnay from a specific area of Burgundy.

### Key Elements

```
CHÂTEAU MARGAUX           ← Producer name (Château = estate)
1er Cru Classé           ← Quality classification (1855 Médoc classification)
MARGAUX                  ← Appellation (the commune / AOC)
APPELLATION MARGAUX CONTRÔLÉE  ← AOC designation
2018                     ← Vintage
MIS EN BOUTEILLE AU CHÂTEAU     ← Estate-bottled (prestigious)
750 ml  13.5% vol
```

### Appellation System (AOC/AOP)

**AOC** = *Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée* (older term, still on bottles)  
**AOP** = *Appellation d'Origine Protégée* (EU harmonized term)

The AOC system controls: geography, permitted grape varieties, maximum yields, minimum alcohol, production methods. The tighter the geographic boundary, typically the higher the quality (and price).

**Hierarchy in Burgundy (from broad to specific):**
```
Bourgogne AOC (regional, broadest)
  ↓
Bourgogne Côte Chalonnaise AOC (sub-regional)
  ↓
Givry AOC (village)
  ↓
Givry Premier Cru AOC (specific vineyard sites within village)
  ↓
[Gevrey-Chambertin] Grand Cru AOC (specific vineyard, highest quality)
```

### IGP and Vin de France

- **IGP** (*Indication Géographique Protégée*): Broader geographic indication — producer has more flexibility on grape variety and methods. Quality varies widely
- **Vin de France:** The most basic category. No appellation. A Vin de France can now state the vintage and grape variety (unlike old Vin de Table rules), which some natural wine producers use intentionally to avoid appellation restrictions

### Useful French Label Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Château | Wine estate (Bordeaux term especially) |
| Domaine | Wine estate (Burgundy/Rhône term) |
| Mis en bouteille au Château/Domaine | Estate-bottled (wine bottled where it was made — more prestigious) |
| Mis en bouteille dans nos caves | Bottled in our cellars (less specific) |
| Vieilles Vignes | Old vines (no legal minimum age, but implies concentrated wine) |
| Clos | Walled vineyard (e.g., Clos Vougeot) |
| Premier Cru (1er Cru) | "First Growth" — in Burgundy this means a specific top vineyard tier; in Bordeaux it means the 5 top châteaux |
| Grand Cru | "Great Growth" — in Burgundy the highest vineyard tier; in Bordeaux the 1855 classification |
| Brut / Extra Brut / Demi-Sec | Champagne/sparkling sweetness levels |
| Négociant | A merchant who buys grapes or wine and makes/sells under their own label |
| Récoltant | Grower who makes their own wine |

## Italy

Italian labels are complex due to the DOC/DOCG system, the many appellations, and the fact that both place and grape can appear on the label depending on the wine.

### Quality Tiers

```
DOCG (Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita)  ← Highest, guaranteed origin
DOC (Denominazione di Origine Controllata)               ← Controlled origin
IGT (Indicazione Geografica Tipica)                      ← Broader geographic indication
Vino d'Italia                                            ← Basic table wine
```

**Confusingly:** Some of Italy's most prestigious and expensive wines are IGT. The "Super Tuscans" (Sassicaia, Ornellaia, Tignanello) were classified as Vino da Tavola or IGT because they used non-traditional grape varieties or methods that fell outside DOC rules. Sassicaia now has its own DOC.

### Reading an Italian Label

```
ANTINORI                    ← Producer
TIGNANELLO                  ← Wine name (a brand/blend name, not a place)
TOSCANA IGT                 ← IGT appellation (Tuscany)
2019                        ← Vintage
750 ml  14% vol
```

Another example (DOCG):
```
GIACOMO CONTERNO            ← Producer
BAROLO                      ← Appellation (DOCG)
CASCINA FRANCIA             ← Specific vineyard (Vigna/MGA designation)
DOCG                        ← Quality classification
2017                        ← Vintage
```

### Aging Terms in Italian Labels

| Term | Applies To | Aging Requirement |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| Normale / Annata | Most DOCs | Minimal or none |
| Superiore | Various | Higher minimum alcohol or longer aging |
| Classico | Chianti, Soave, etc. | Historic core zone |
| Riserva | Many DOCGs | Extended aging (varies — usually 2–5 years total) |
| Gran Selezione | Chianti Classico only | Min. 30 months, single vineyard |

### FVG Labels Specifically

Friuli wines often show both the appellation and the grape:
```
LIVIO FELLUGA                ← Producer
TERRE ALTE                   ← Blend name
COLLI ORIENTALI DEL FRIULI DOC  ← Appellation
ROSAZZO                      ← Sub-zone (Rosazzo is also a DOCG for Friulano)
2020                         ← Vintage
```

Or simpler:
```
EDI SIMČIČ                   ← Producer
RIBOLLA GIALLA               ← Grape variety
COLLIO DOC                   ← Appellation
2021                         ← Vintage
```

## Spain

Spanish labels combine place and grape in different ways depending on the region. Rioja is famous for its aging classifications on the label.

### Appellation Hierarchy

```
DOCa (Denominación de Origen Calificada)  ← Highest (only Rioja and Priorat)
DO (Denominación de Origen)               ← Most quality wines
IGP (Indicación Geográfica Protegida)     ← Broader
Vino de España                            ← Basic
```

### Rioja Aging Labels (appears on the back label capsule)

| Classification | Minimum Aging | Oak Minimum |
|----------------|---------------|-------------|
| Joven | No minimum | No minimum |
| Crianza | 2 years total | 1 year in oak |
| Reserva | 3 years total | 1 year in oak |
| Gran Reserva | 5 years total | 2 years in oak |

```
MARQUÉS DE RISCAL          ← Producer
RIOJA RESERVA              ← Appellation + aging tier
DENOMINACIÓN DE ORIGEN CALIFICADA  ← Classification
2018                       ← Vintage
13.5% vol  750 ml
```

## Germany

German labels carry the most information of any country — but also the most complex system.

### Quality Tiers

```
Prädikatswein (highest)
  ├── Kabinett
  ├── Spätlese
  ├── Auslese
  ├── Beerenauslese (BA)
  ├── Eiswein
  └── Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA)
Qualitätswein (QbA)
Landwein
Deutscher Wein (basic)
```

The Prädikat levels indicate harvest ripeness (when grapes were picked), which correlates to sweetness potential — but NOT necessarily actual sweetness of the final wine. *Trocken* (dry) on the label overrides the Prädikat to tell you the wine is dry.

### Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Trocken | Dry |
| Halbtrocken / Feinherb | Off-dry |
| Süß | Sweet |
| GG (Grosses Gewächs) | Dry wine from a top-classified vineyard (VDP classification) |
| Erste Lage (1er Lage) | First-classified vineyard site |
| VDP | Verband Deutscher Prädikatsweingüter — Germany's top wine producer association; their classification is reliable |
| Weingut | Wine estate |
| Weinkellerei | Wine cellar/winery |
| Erzeugerabfüllung | Estate-bottled |

Example:
```
DR. LOOSEN                  ← Producer (Weingut)
ERDENER TREPPCHEN           ← Vineyard name (Erdener = village, Treppchen = vineyard)
RIESLING SPÄTLESE           ← Grape + Prädikat level
MOSEL                       ← Region
2020                        ← Vintage
8.5% vol                    ← Low alcohol = likely off-dry or sweet
```

## Austria

Similar to Germany but with important differences. The VDA (Vinea Wachau Nobilis Districtus) for the Wachau defines three ripeness tiers:

| Tier | Alcohol | Style |
|------|---------|-------|
| Steinfeder | max 11% | Light, delicate |
| Federspiel | 11–12.5% | Classic dry table wine |
| Smaragd | 12.5%+ | Rich, full-bodied dry |

Named after local animals (straw spider, falcon, emerald lizard). 

DAC (*Districtus Austriae Controllatus*) is Austria's controlled appellation system.

## Slovenia

Slovenia uses an EU-aligned appellation system with two tiers:

- **ZGP** (*Zaščitena geografska poreklo* / PDO): Protected Designation of Origin — the highest tier. Strictly defined geographic areas with approved grapes and production rules. Examples: ZGP Goriška Brda, ZGP Vipavska Dolina, ZGP Kras
- **PGO** (*Zaščitena geografska označba* / PGI): Protected Geographic Indication — broader, more flexible

Slovenian labels typically show: producer name, wine name or grape variety, vintage, ZGP appellation.

**Quality categories within ZGP:**
- *Vrhunsko vino* — Premium quality wine (the best tier)
- *Kakovostno vino* — Quality wine
- *Deželno vino* — Regional wine (PGO level)

Example:
```
MOVIA                       ← Producer (klet = cellar/winery)
REBULA                      ← Grape variety (= Ribolla Gialla)
BRDA                        ← Sub-region
ZGP GORIŠKA BRDA            ← Appellation
2020                        ← Vintage
VRHUNSKO VINO               ← Quality tier
PRIDELANO V SLOVENIJI       ← "Produced in Slovenia"
```

### Slovenian Back Label

Slovenian wine law requires extensive back-label information including:
- Organoleptic description (color, aroma, taste notes — mandatory)
- Serving temperature recommendation
- Suggested food pairings
- Producer contact details

This makes Slovenian labels unusually informative.

## New World Labels

New World labels are the simplest to read. They lead with brand and grape, not place.

```
PENFOLDS                    ← Brand
BIN 389                     ← Wine name
CABERNET SHIRAZ             ← Grape varieties (blend order)
SOUTH AUSTRALIA             ← Geographic origin (broad)
2021                        ← Vintage
750 ml  14.5% vol
```

The geographic indication can be broad (South Australia = huge area) or specific (Barossa Valley, Coonawarra). More specific = smaller area = typically higher quality.

**US AVA** (*American Viticultural Area*): The US equivalent of appellation. Labels showing a specific AVA (Napa Valley, Russian River Valley) must use 85% grapes from that area.

---

## Anatomy of a Back Label

Front labels have the brand/prestige information. Back labels are often more useful:

- **Tasting notes** (often marketing, but give style expectations)
- **Technical data:** Residual sugar, total acidity, pH
- **Food pairings**
- **Production notes** (grape varieties, aging, winemaking)
- **Importer information** (useful for tracing origin)
- **Harvest date, grape source**

## Common Label Traps

| Label claim | Reality |
|-------------|---------|
| "Reserve" | Legally meaningless in most New World countries. Can mean anything or nothing |
| "Old Vines" | No legal definition. Could mean 15-year-old vines |
| Gold medal / award | Competitions vary wildly in quality and rigor |
| "Handcrafted" / "Artisanal" | Marketing language with no legal definition |
| High alcohol (15%+) | Not a quality signal — just a stylistic choice or warm climate |
| Complex back-label | Not correlated with wine quality |
| Heavyweight bottle | Heavy glass is a cost signal, not a quality signal |

## Self-Quiz

1. What does "mis en bouteille au Château" mean on a Bordeaux label?
2. A German Riesling says "Spätlese Trocken." What do you know about this wine?
3. A Rioja label says "Gran Reserva." What does that tell you about aging?
4. What is the difference between DOC and DOCG in Italy?
5. What is ZGP on a Slovenian label and how does it compare to Italy's DOCG?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. "Bottled at the estate." The wine was not only grown and made at the property, but also bottled there — it never left the estate before being sealed. This is a prestige signal: it guarantees the wine wasn't blended or altered by a merchant after leaving the winery.

2. Spätlese means the grapes were harvested late (riper than normal). Trocken means the wine is dry — fermented out to very low residual sugar despite the ripe grapes. The result is typically a full-bodied, dry Riesling with good concentration and high acidity, not a sweet wine.

3. Minimum 5 years of total aging (at least 2 years in oak, the rest in bottle) before release. Gran Reserva is only produced in the best years, from selected top plots. It is the longest-aged and most structured Rioja tier.

4. DOC (*Denominazione di Origine Controllata*) is the standard controlled appellation. DOCG (*Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*) adds a "guaranteed" element — wines are tasted and approved by a tasting committee before release. In practice, DOCG covers Italy's most prestigious wines (Barolo, Brunello, Chianti Classico, Amarone, etc.) with stricter rules on grapes, yields, and aging.

5. ZGP (*Zaščitena geografska poreklo*) is Slovenia's Protected Designation of Origin — the equivalent of Italy's DOC/DOCG or France's AOC. It defines the geography, permitted grapes, and production rules. Within ZGP, *Vrhunsko vino ZGP* (premium quality wine) is the highest tier — roughly comparable to DOCG in function.

</details>

Pick five wines from your shelf or wine shop and decode every element on the label using this module. For each bottle, identify: producer, appellation/region, grape variety (if stated or known), vintage, quality tier, and any aging indication. You should feel comfortable doing this for French, Italian, Slovenian, and one New World wine.
