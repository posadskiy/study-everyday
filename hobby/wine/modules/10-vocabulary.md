# Module 10 — Wine Vocabulary & Conversation

This module does two things: provides a comprehensive glossary of wine terms, and teaches you how to use language confidently in real-world wine situations — shops, restaurants, tastings, and conversation.

---

## Part 1: The Wine Vocabulary

Terms are grouped by context. Each definition explains the concept and, where relevant, explains how to use it in conversation.

---

### Structural Terms (What You Taste)

**Acidity**  
The tartness or crispness in a wine, detected on the sides of the tongue and under the jaw (where it triggers salivation). Wines with high acidity feel "lively" or "fresh." Low acidity wines can feel "flat" or "flabby." Acidity is a preservative and essential for aging.  
*Use it:* "This Riesling has beautiful acidity — very mouthwatering."

**Tannin**  
Polyphenolic compounds from grape skins, seeds, and stems (also from oak). They create a drying, astringent, gripping sensation in the mouth. Tannins are only found in red wines (and orange wines). They soften with bottle age.  
*Use it:* "Young Barolo is hard to enjoy because the tannins are so gripping — you need 10 years."

**Body**  
The weight and fullness of a wine in the mouth. Light-bodied wines feel like water; full-bodied wines feel like whole milk. Driven by alcohol, glycerol, residual sugar.  
*Use it:* "This is a medium-bodied red, quite accessible."

**Residual Sugar (RS)**  
Grape sugar that remains unfermented after winemaking. Expressed in g/L. Dry wine < 4 g/L. Off-dry 4–12 g/L. Medium 12–45 g/L. Sweet 45+ g/L. You cannot always taste sweetness directly — perceived sweetness is moderated by acidity.

**Alcohol (ABV)**  
Alcohol by volume. Felt as warmth in the throat. Wines with very high alcohol (15%+) can taste "hot" if not balanced by fruit and acidity.

**Balance**  
When all structural elements (acidity, tannin, alcohol, sweetness, fruit) work in harmony rather than one dominating. The hallmark of a great wine.  
*Use it:* "Despite the high tannins, the fruit and acidity keep it balanced."

**Finish / Length**  
How long flavors persist after swallowing. Short (<5 sec), medium (5–15 sec), long (15–30 sec), very long (30+). One of the clearest quality indicators.  
*Use it:* "The finish is remarkable — it just keeps going."

**Complexity**  
Multiple layers of flavor and aroma that evolve in the glass and on the palate. A complex wine offers more to discover the longer you smell and taste it.

**Structure**  
The architecture of a wine — the combination of acidity, tannin, and alcohol. A "structured" wine is typically high in at least two of these, usually tannic and acidic, suggesting aging potential.

### Descriptive Terms (Character and Style)

**Austere**  
Lean, tight, minimal obvious fruit. Often used for young wines or classic European styles (young Chablis, young Barolo). Not negative — austere wines often develop beautifully.

**Backward**  
A wine that is not yet showing its potential. Needs more time.  
*Use it:* "This 2018 Burgundy is very backward — come back in five years."

**Brooding**  
Dark, deep, somewhat withdrawn. Often used for powerful young reds — Barolo, big Syrah.

**Closed**  
A wine that is not releasing aromas readily. Often happens after a wine has been recently shipped (bottle shock) or during a developmental phase. Will often open with aeration or time.

**Corked**  
Affected by TCA (trichloroanisole) from a contaminated cork. Smells of wet cardboard, musty basement, damp cloth. Masks all fruit. A wine fault.  
*Use it (in a restaurant):* "I think this bottle may be corked — it smells of damp cardboard."

**Crisp**  
High, refreshing acidity. Common positive descriptor for whites.

**Earthy**  
A wine with soil, mushroom, truffle, or other non-fruity terroir-related aromas. Positive in context. Classic in Burgundy, Barolo, old-vine French country reds.

**Elegant**  
Refined, not heavy or overblown. Associated with restraint, finesse, complexity without power.

**Fat**  
Full-bodied, low in acidity, rich texture. Can be positive (oaked Chardonnay, Grenache) or negative if used to imply lack of freshness.

**Flinty / Mineral**  
A stony, chalky, or gunflint-like aroma/taste. Common in Chablis, Loire Sauvignon Blanc, white Burgundy, Vitovska. Disputed as a scientifically definable category but widely used and understood.

**Flabby**  
Insufficient acidity — the wine feels flat and lifeless. Negative.

**Forward**  
Showing its fruit and character readily, drinking well now. Opposite of backward.

**Fresh**  
High acidity, vibrant, lively. Almost always positive.

**Grip**  
Tannin or acidity that gives the wine structure. "A wine with great grip" means well-structured, with substance.

**Herbaceous**  
Green herb character (grass, green pepper, tomato leaf). Natural in some varieties (Sauvignon Blanc, Cabernet Franc); a sign of underripeness in others (Cabernet Sauvignon with strong green pepper).

**Hot**  
Too much perceived alcohol — burning sensation in the finish. A fault or stylistic mismatch.

**Lean**  
Low fruit weight, angular, minimal flesh on the bones. Can be positive (elegant) or negative (thin).

**Lush**  
Soft, ripe, generous fruit. Usually positive for accessible, easy-drinking wines.

**Opulent**  
Extravagantly rich and generous. Used for very ripe, full wines — California Cabernet, Amarone.

**Precise**  
Focused, defined aromas and flavors — nothing fuzzy or vague. A quality indicator.  
*Use it:* "The Collio Friulano is very precise — citrus, almond, completely clean."

**Reductive**  
Wine showing struck-match, rubber, or sulfur aromas due to very limited oxygen contact during winemaking. Often dissipates with aeration.

**Rustic**  
Rough edges — elevated volatile acidity, slight brett, coarse tannins. Not necessarily bad — many traditional wines are positively rustic.

**Silky**  
Smooth, fine-grained tannin that glides over the palate. The holy grail of aged Pinot Noir and great Barolo.

**Supple**  
Soft, flexible tannin. Gives the wine a round, approachable texture.

**Tight**  
Closed, concentrated, not fully open. Similar to backward/closed. A tight wine needs air or time.

**Toasty**  
Oak-derived aromas of toast, smoke, brioche. Common in barrel-aged wines and aged Champagne.

**Velvety**  
Even smoother than silky — seamless, luxurious texture. Rare and impressive.

**Volatile Acidity (VA)**  
Acetic acid (vinegar) character in wine. In low amounts, adds complexity. In high amounts, a fault. When you smell nail polish remover or sharp vinegar, that's high VA.

### Winemaking and Classification Terms

**Appellation**  
A legally defined geographic area with specific wine production rules. Broader appellation = more flexibility, usually lower quality floor. Specific appellation = tighter rules, usually higher quality floor.

**AVA**  
American Viticultural Area — the US appellation system.

**Biodynamic**  
A farming philosophy that treats the vineyard as a complete ecosystem, following the lunar calendar for treatments and harvesting. Certification by Demeter. No synthetic chemicals. Stricter than organic.

**Blanc de Blancs**  
Sparkling wine (usually Champagne) made exclusively from white grapes (Chardonnay). Typically leaner, more citrus-driven.

**Blanc de Noirs**  
Sparkling wine made from red-skinned grapes (Pinot Noir, Pinot Meunier) but processed without skin contact, producing a white or very pale wine.

**Botrytis (Noble Rot)**  
*Botrytis cinerea* — a fungus that, under the right conditions, concentrates sugars in grapes by dehydrating them. Produces the world's great sweet wines: Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú, German BA/TBA, late-harvest Riesling. Under wrong conditions it's just grey rot and ruins the crop.

**Brut**  
Dry style in sparkling wine. < 12 g/L residual sugar.  
Extra Brut: < 6 g/L. Brut Nature / Zero Dosage: < 3 g/L.

**Carbonic Maceration**  
Fermentation technique where whole, uncrushed grapes ferment inside themselves in CO₂. Produces very fruity, low-tannin, soft red wine. Classic for Beaujolais (Gamay).

**Cru**  
French for "growth" — can mean a specific vineyard (Burgundy) or a classified estate (Bordeaux).

**Dosage**  
Sugar added to Champagne/sparkling wine just before final corking to adjust sweetness level and balance the acidity from bottle fermentation.

**En primeur**  
Buying wine futures — purchasing before the wine is bottled and released, at a discount, trusting the wine will be as described. Common for Bordeaux.

**Flor**  
A layer of yeast that grows on the surface of aging Fino and Manzanilla Sherry, protecting it from oxidation and contributing distinctive nutty, salty, briny character.

**Horizontal tasting**  
Tasting the same vintage across multiple producers. Illustrates terroir differences.

**Lees**  
Dead yeast cells and grape solids left after fermentation. Aging wine "on lees" (*sur lie*) adds texture, richness, and complexity (especially in white wines and Champagne).

**Maceration**  
Skin contact during fermentation (reds) or intentional skin contact for white wines (orange wine technique).

**Malolactic Fermentation (MLF)**  
Bacterial conversion of sharp malic acid to soft lactic acid. Softens acidity, adds buttery texture. See Module 4.

**Méthode Traditionnelle / Champenoise**  
Traditional method sparkling wine — secondary fermentation in bottle. See Module 4.

**Natural Wine**  
No single legal definition. Loosely means: organically grown grapes, indigenous yeast fermentation, no or minimal additions (sulfur in very low doses only), no fining or filtration. See Module 12.

**Négociant**  
A merchant who buys grapes or bulk wine and produces wine under their own label. Can range from large commercial operations to very small quality-focused houses.

**Non-Vintage (NV)**  
Wine blended from multiple years, especially Champagne. Maintains house style consistency.

**Orange Wine**  
White wine made with extended skin contact. See Module 4.

**Organic**  
Grapes grown without synthetic pesticides, herbicides, or chemical fertilizers. EU certification available. Does not necessarily mean natural winemaking in the cellar.

**Oxidation**  
Deliberate (Sherry, Madeira, Tawny Port — positive) or accidental (wine left open too long or poorly sealed — fault).

**Pétillant Naturel (Pét-Nat)**  
Ancestral method sparkling wine — bottled before primary fermentation complete. See Module 4.

**Residual Sugar (RS)**  
Sugar remaining after fermentation. See structural terms above.

**Solera**  
Fractional blending aging system used in Sherry and Tawny Port. Barrels are stacked; wine is drawn from the oldest barrel (bottom tier) and topped up from younger barrels.

**Sur lie**  
Aging wine on its lees (dead yeast). Adds richness, texture, and complexity. Essential for Muscadet Sèvre et Maine, Champagne.

**Terroir**  
The complete natural environment: soil, climate, aspect, altitude, and how they express themselves in wine. See Module 1.

**Vendange tardive**  
"Late harvest" in French (Alsace). Grapes picked very ripe, producing rich, often sweet wine.

**Vertical tasting**  
Tasting multiple vintages of the same wine. Illustrates vintage variation and aging.

**Vieilles Vignes**  
"Old vines" in French. No legal minimum age. Typically implies more concentrated wine.

**Viticulture**  
The science and practice of growing wine grapes. What happens in the vineyard.

**Viniculture (Oenology / Enology)**  
The science and practice of making wine. What happens in the cellar.

## Part 2: How to Talk About Wine

Knowing vocabulary is one thing. Using it naturally is another. Here are the real-world situations you'll encounter.

### In a Wine Shop

**How to ask for a recommendation:**
> "I'm looking for something under €25. I tend to enjoy wines with good acidity — I've liked Friulano and Sauvignon Blanc recently. What would you suggest?"

Be specific about:
- Budget (always)
- Style you like (fresh vs. rich, red vs. white, etc.)
- Occasion (dinner party, aperitivo, pairing with a specific dish)
- What you've enjoyed recently

Avoid: "Something nice." Shopkeepers are not mind readers.

**How to ask about an unfamiliar wine:**
> "I'm curious about this Vitovska — can you tell me about it? What does it pair well with?"

**How to admit you don't know:**
> "I'm just starting to explore Slovenian wine — what would you recommend to get a sense of the region?"

Honest admission of learning is welcomed by good wine shop staff, not judged.

### In a Restaurant

**How to use a wine list:**
1. Note the occasion and budget first
2. Look at the wine list with the menu — pair to the food, not the other way round
3. Find the by-the-glass list if you want to taste
4. Look for the sommelier's specials or "lesser-known gems" section — these are often the best value

**How to communicate budget discreetly:**
> "Could you recommend something in this range?" (pointing to a mid-range price on the list, without saying the number aloud)

This is the classic sommelier communication technique and any good sommelier will understand.

**How to ask for help without feeling embarrassed:**
> "We're having the fish and the lamb — what do you suggest to pair with both?"

**How to handle an unfamiliar wine list:**
> "I don't know this producer — what's the style? Is it more like a Burgundy or more New World in character?"

### At a Tasting Event

**If asked your opinion on a wine:**
> "I find it very precise — high acidity, some minerality. I'd guess it's from a cool climate. The tannins are fine-grained, not overpowering."

Describe what you observe. You don't need to guess the grape or region confidently if you don't know.

**If you don't like a wine:**
> "It's not quite my style — I tend to prefer more acidity. But I can see the quality."

You can acknowledge quality without pretending to love something.

**If someone uses terms you don't recognize:**
> "When you say it's 'reductive,' what does that mean exactly in this context?"

Asking for clarification is a sign of engagement, not ignorance.

### Common Phrases That Sound Confident

Instead of... | Say...
--- | ---
"I like it" | "I'm finding it quite approachable — the tannins are soft."
"It's too strong for me" | "It's quite full-bodied — I usually prefer something with more acidity."
"It smells kind of weird" | "It's quite reductive — I think it needs some air."
"I don't know what I'm tasting" | "I'm picking up some fruit but it's quite closed right now."
"It's sour" | "Very high acidity — quite bracing."
"It tastes expensive" | "Good complexity and a really long finish."
"This is bad" | "I think this bottle may be corked."
"I've never heard of this" | "I haven't tried wines from this region before — what should I expect?"

### Describing FVG and Slovenian Wines Specifically

A few reliable phrases for talking about local wines:

- **On Friulano:** "Very characteristic — that bitter almond note on the finish is the giveaway. Dry, precise, very food-friendly."
- **On Ribolla Gialla (conventional):** "High acidity, quite lean and citrus-driven. Very refreshing."
- **On orange wine (Gravner, Radikon, Movia):** "It's a skin-contact wine — the tannin structure is unusual for a white. Amber color, oxidative notes, complex. It pairs like a light red rather than a white."
- **On Vitovska:** "Almost exclusively from the Carso. Very mineral, saline — you can almost taste the limestone and sea air. Quite unique."
- **On Teran:** "Dense, almost inky red. Very high in polyphenols — intensely tannic and acidic. A serious food wine."

## Self-Quiz

1. What does "backward" mean when used to describe a wine, and what does it imply about when to drink it?
2. Explain the difference between "tannin" and "acidity" as structural components.
3. How would you ask a sommelier for a wine recommendation within your budget discreetly?
4. What is the correct way to reject a wine at a restaurant?
5. Describe a Friulano using at least four vocabulary terms from this module.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Backward means the wine is not yet showing its potential — it is closed, tight, not releasing aromas or complexity. It implies you should wait: cellar it for more years, or at minimum decant it for a couple of hours. Drinking it now would be disappointing relative to what it will become.

2. Tannin is a texture — a drying, gripping, mouth-coating sensation from polyphenols (found in grape skins and oak). It's only in red and orange wines. Acidity is a taste — the sharp, mouthwatering, salivating sensation from acids in the wine (malic, tartaric, lactic). Both whites and reds have acidity. Together, tannin and acidity form the "structure" — the backbone that allows fine wines to age.

3. Point to a price on the wine list without saying the number aloud: "We're looking for something in this range." Every professional sommelier understands this convention immediately and will not find it awkward.

4. Only reject a wine that is genuinely faulty (corked, oxidized, volatile). Describe what you observe: "I think this bottle may be corked — it has a damp cardboard smell." Do not reject a wine because you don't enjoy the style or it tastes different from what you expected — those are not faults.

5. Example: "Pale straw in color. Precise and clean on the nose — white peach, hay, a note of almond. On the palate it's medium-bodied, fresh, high acidity, completely dry. There's a characteristic bitter almond finish that's quite elegant. Medium-long finish. Classic and austere in the best sense."
