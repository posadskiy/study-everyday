# Module 1 — The World of Wine

## What Is Wine?

Wine is fermented grape juice. That sentence is both completely accurate and completely insufficient — which is the whole point of this course.

Grapes contain water, sugar, and acids. When yeast consumes the sugars, it produces alcohol and carbon dioxide. What remains is wine. The process is ancient, arguably accidental in origin, and has been refined over thousands of years into one of the most complex beverages humans make.

What makes wine interesting is not the fermentation itself — that's chemistry. What makes it interesting is everything that shapes the grape before fermentation begins: **the variety, the soil, the climate, the slope, the year, and the decisions of the person who grew and made it.** Each of those variables leaves a fingerprint in the glass.

## A Brief History

**~6000 BCE — Georgia and the Caucasus.** The oldest evidence of deliberate winemaking. Residue in clay vessels from the region that is now Georgia.

**~4000 BCE — Ancient Near East.** Wine spreads through Mesopotamia and Egypt. It is traded, taxed, and features in religious ritual.

**~800 BCE — Greece.** Greek colonists carry viticulture west into Southern France and Italy. The word "wine" traces back through Latin *vinum* to Greek *oinos* to a Proto-Indo-European root.

**Roman Empire.** Romans systematize viticulture across Europe — Bordeaux, Burgundy, the Rhine, the Moselle, the Danube. Many of today's great wine regions exist because Romans planted vines there.

**Medieval Europe.** The Church preserves viticulture through monasteries. Benedictine and Cistercian monks in Burgundy develop the concept that specific plots of land produce distinct wines — the early idea of *terroir*.

**17th–18th century.** Glass bottles and cork stoppers make long-distance wine trade possible. Bordeaux and Champagne emerge as commercial powerhouses. The concept of "vintage" wine — wine from a specific year — becomes meaningful.

**19th century — Phylloxera.** A root louse from North America devastates European vineyards. Almost every vine in Europe is destroyed. Recovery takes decades and reshapes the industry permanently. The solution — grafting European vines onto American rootstock — is still standard practice today.

**20th century.** California, Australia, Argentina, and New Zealand develop serious wine industries. Technology (temperature-controlled fermentation, stainless steel tanks) makes consistently good wine possible at scale. Wine criticism emerges as a profession. The 100-point scoring system is introduced.

**21st century.** Natural wine, orange wine, and minimal-intervention winemaking challenge the orthodoxy. Climate change alters growing seasons across Europe. Regions previously considered too cold (England, parts of Scandinavia) begin producing viable wine.

## Old World vs New World

This is the most useful rough framework for understanding wine culture.

| | Old World | New World |
|---|---|---|
| **Where** | Europe, North Africa, Middle East | Americas, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa |
| **Label focus** | Place (Bordeaux, Chianti, Chablis) | Grape variety (Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon) |
| **Style tendency** | More restrained, lower alcohol, higher acidity, earthy | More fruit-forward, higher alcohol, rounder |
| **Regulation** | Strict — what grapes, yields, methods are permitted | Flexible — producers choose their own approach |
| **Attitude** | Tradition, terroir expression | Innovation, consumer accessibility |

These are generalizations with many exceptions. An Australian winemaker making Pinot Noir in a Burgundian style breaks this mold. A Californian natural wine producer rejects New World orthodoxy entirely. Use this framework as a starting orientation, not a rule.

## Terroir

*Terroir* is the French word for "land" but in wine it means something broader: **the complete natural environment in which a vine grows**, and how that environment expresses itself in the wine.

Terroir encompasses:

**Soil.** Limestone retains water and reflects heat; it tends to produce wines with high acidity and minerality (Burgundy, Champagne, Chablis). Clay holds water and warmth; it produces richer, more structured wines (Right Bank Bordeaux). Slate drains quickly and heats up fast; it's associated with aromatic Rieslings (Mosel). Volcanic soils (Etna in Sicily, Santorini in Greece) tend toward intense, salty, mineral wines. The Carso/Kras plateau — which you'll encounter locally — is the opposite of volcanic: it's dense Cretaceous limestone (karst) with pockets of terra rossa (iron-rich red clay). This gives local wines like Vitovska and Teran their distinctive stony, saline character.

**Climate.** Cool climates produce grapes with higher natural acidity and lower sugar (and therefore lower potential alcohol). Warm climates produce riper, richer fruit. The difference between Burgundy (cool) and Napa Valley (warm) shows in every glass.

**Aspect and altitude.** A slope facing south gets more sun. Higher elevation means cooler temperatures and slower ripening. Both affect the flavor profile.

**Microclimate.** A river nearby moderates temperature and humidity. A forest on the north side blocks cold winds. A specific valley creates a funnel for morning fog that cools the vines in summer. These micro-level variations are why two adjacent vineyards owned by the same producer can taste different.

Some critics argue terroir is overused — a convenient story to justify high prices for land in famous appellations. The concept is worth questioning, but the underlying observation is hard to dismiss: wines from specific places have a consistent character that repeats across producers, vintages, and years. A Chablis from a top-rated vineyard reliably tastes leaner, more mineral, and more acidic than a Chardonnay from the warmer Mâconnais 100 km south — not sometimes, but every time, across dozens of producers. That repeatable character is what terroir means in practice. The cause is real even if the word has been abused.

## Vintage

A vintage is the year the grapes were harvested. Every wine label bears one (except non-vintage wines like most Champagne, Fino Sherry, and blended spirits-style products).

The vintage matters because weather varies. A cool, wet summer may mean underripe grapes with thin flavors. A warm, dry year with rain at just the right moments may produce exceptional, concentrated fruit. These differences are baked into the wine permanently.

This is why the same producer's same wine can cost €20 in one year and €80 in another. Burgundy 2010 and 2015 are considered exceptional; 2011 and 2013 are considered ordinary. Same vineyards, same winemakers, very different wines and prices.

Vintage matters more in marginal climates (Burgundy, Mosel, Champagne, Piedmont) than in consistently warm ones (Southern Italy, many parts of California, Argentina). In warmer climates the variation between years is smaller.

## The Wine Pyramid

Not all wine is equal, and price reflects a rough quality hierarchy — though marketing, brand, and fashion distort it significantly.

```
            ┌──────────────────┐
            │  Icon / Prestige │   ← First-growth Bordeaux, Grand Cru Burgundy,
            │     Wines        │     Barolo from great vintages, cult producers
            └──────────────────┘
           ┌────────────────────┐
           │  Premium & Fine    │   ← Village-level Burgundy, Premier Cru, top
           │     Wines          │     estates from good appellations
           └────────────────────┘
          ┌──────────────────────┐
          │  Quality Everyday    │   ← Good appellation wines, reliable producers,
          │     Wines            │     regional DOC/DOCGs
          └──────────────────────┘
         ┌────────────────────────┐
         │  Basic Table Wine /    │   ← IGT, Vin de France, generic varietals,
         │  Commercial Wine       │     supermarket own-label
         └────────────────────────┘
```

The interesting insight is that the "quality everyday" tier is where most value lives. Icon wines are priced for collectors and status signals. A €20 bottle from a well-chosen appellation routinely outperforms a €60 bottle purchased without knowledge.

## Self-Quiz

1. What does *terroir* mean and what does it include?
2. What is the main difference between how Old World and New World wines are labeled?
3. Why does the vintage year matter on a wine bottle?
4. What destroyed most of Europe's vineyards in the 19th century, and how was it solved?
5. What is wine, at the most basic level?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The complete natural environment where a vine grows: soil type, climate (temperature, rainfall), aspect (slope direction), altitude, and microclimate. It expresses itself as consistent character in the wine from a specific place.

2. Old World labels lead with the place (Chablis, Chianti, Rioja). New World labels lead with the grape variety (Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon, Sauvignon Blanc). You need to know which grape grows in a given Old World appellation; New World tells you directly.

3. Because weather varies year to year — temperature, rainfall, sunshine. These affect grape ripeness, acidity, and flavor. A great vintage from a good producer can be significantly better (and more expensive) than the same producer's wine from a difficult year.

4. Phylloxera, a root louse from North America. It was solved by grafting European vines (*Vitis vinifera*) onto American rootstock, which is resistant to the louse. Almost all European vines today still grow on American roots.

5. Fermented grape juice. Yeast converts the sugars in grape juice into alcohol and carbon dioxide.

## Practical Exercise

**Do this after reading Module 6 (Tasting), not necessarily right now.**

The purpose is to make the Old World vs New World concept concrete, not to demonstrate tasting skill you haven't yet built. Once you've read Module 6, come back and do this:

Buy two bottles of the same grape variety — one from a classic Old World region, one from a New World region. A good starting pair: a French Sauvignon Blanc (Sancerre, Pouilly-Fumé, or a basic Bordeaux Blanc) and a New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc (Marlborough). Taste them side by side. You don't need to identify every aroma — just notice: which smells sharper and greener? Which smells riper and fruitier? Which feels more acidic in your mouth? Which one would you rather drink on a hot day vs with food? These differences are the Old World / New World contrast in practice.
