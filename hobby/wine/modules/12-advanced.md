## Natural Wine

Natural wine has no single legal definition, but the community broadly agrees on core principles:

**In the vineyard:**
- Organically or biodynamically farmed grapes
- No synthetic pesticides, herbicides, or fungicides
- Hand harvesting
- No irrigation (in most cases)

**In the cellar:**
- Indigenous/wild yeast fermentation only (no commercial yeasts)
- No or minimal sulfur dioxide (SO₂) additions — many natural wines have no added sulfur at all
- No fining agents (egg whites, isinglass, bentonite)
- No filtration, or very light filtration
- No addition of tannins, acids, enzymes, or other enological additives
- No concentration techniques (no reverse osmosis, vacuum evaporation)

**The result:** Wine that is more unpredictable, more alive, potentially more complex — and also sometimes more unstable, slightly funky, or cloudy. Natural wine lovers embrace this variability; critics argue it leads to faulty wine sold at premium prices.

### Natural vs. Organic vs. Biodynamic

| Term | Legal Definition? | What It Guarantees |
|------|------------------|--------------------|
| Organic | Yes (EU certified) | No synthetic farming inputs |
| Biodynamic | Yes (Demeter certified) | Organic + lunar calendar + holistic farm ecosystem |
| Natural | No | Nothing legally; philosophy-based |

A wine can be organic in the vineyard but heavily interventionist in the cellar. A natural wine can be brilliant or faulty. These terms are not interchangeable.

### Natural Wine and FVG/Slovenia

The Collio/Brda border zone is one of the spiritual homes of the natural wine movement. Gravner and Radikon pioneered not just skin-contact wine but a radical reduction of cellar intervention. Their influence spread through Slovenia (Movia, Batič, Burja, Čotar) and globally.

Today, every young winemaker in the region is influenced by this legacy. You can't understand wines from this area without understanding the natural wine conversation.

## Organic and Biodynamic Viticulture

**Organic farming** eliminates synthetic chemicals. Permitted treatments include: copper sulfate (the traditional "Bordeaux mixture" against mildew), sulfur (against mildew and spider mites), plant-based preparations.

**Biodynamic farming** (Demeter certification) treats the farm as a self-sustaining organism. It draws on Rudolf Steiner's 1924 lectures. Key elements:
- The biodynamic calendar divides days into "fruit," "root," "flower," and "leaf" days based on lunar and stellar positions; winemakers taste on "fruit days"
- Preparations (numbered 500–508) made from cow manure, herbs, and minerals, applied in homeopathic quantities
- No synthetic inputs of any kind

**Evidence:** Organic and biodynamic viticulture produce measurably healthier soils with more biodiversity. Whether the biodynamic calendar specifically affects wine quality is scientifically disputed. What is not disputed: the most meticulous producers in the world (DRC, Leroy, Chapoutier, Becker, Zind-Humbrecht, many Collio/Brda estates) are biodynamic or at minimum organic.

## Wine Collecting and Cellaring

### Why Collect?

1. **Access to aged wine:** Most fine wine is released too young to drink. Collecting lets you taste wine at its peak
2. **Value:** Buying en primeur or at release and cellaring is often cheaper than buying the same wine at peak drinking age
3. **Discovery:** Building a cellar teaches you about aging, development, and vintage variation in a way reading never can

### What to Collect

Not all wine benefits from aging. Most wine is made to be drunk within 1–5 years. Wine worth cellaring has:
- **High tannin (reds):** Gives structure for the long haul. Barolo, Brunello, serious Bordeaux, Northern Rhône Syrah
- **High acidity:** The preservative element. Essential for whites (Riesling, Burgundy whites) and reds (Nebbiolo, Sangiovese)
- **Quality producer:** Great terroir and winemaking is required. A mediocre wine doesn't become great with age — it just becomes old
- **Good vintage:** A poor vintage usually doesn't age into a good wine

**Collectible wine categories:**
- Red Burgundy Grand Cru and Premier Cru (age 10–30 years)
- Barolo and Barbaresco (age 10–25 years)
- Brunello di Montalcino (age 10–20 years)
- Serious Bordeaux (age 10–30 years)
- Northern Rhône Syrah — Hermitage, Côte-Rôtie (age 10–20 years)
- German Riesling Auslese/BA/TBA (age 10–50 years)
- Champagne vintage and prestige cuvées (age 10–25 years)
- Sauternes and Tokaji (age 10–50 years)
- **FVG: Gravner and Radikon** (release at 5–7 years, age another 15–25)

### Cellar Conditions

- Temperature: 12–14°C, consistent (fluctuation is the enemy)
- Humidity: 60–80%
- Darkness
- No vibration
- Bottles horizontal (natural cork contact with wine)

**Wine fridge** (not a kitchen fridge — the temperature is too low and fluctuates when opened): A temperature-controlled wine storage unit. Entry models from €150–300 hold 12–24 bottles. Essential for serious storage if you don't have a cellar.

### CellarTracker

Free software/app (cellartracker.com). The global wine collector community logs what they have, when they bought it, and tasting notes.

Useful for:
- Tracking your own collection with drink windows
- Reading community tasting notes on mainstream international wines (Burgundy, Barolo, Bordeaux, etc.)
- Seeing how a wine develops across multiple vintages

**Limitation:** Coverage of Slovenian and small FVG producers is very thin — even well-known Brda or Collio estates may have only a handful of entries. For this region, **Wine-Searcher** is more useful for producer research.

## Wine Investment

Wine as an investment is a real market, but one that requires knowledge to navigate.

### How It Works

Fine wine (mainly Bordeaux First Growths, Burgundy Grand Cru, a handful of Rhône and Italian icons) is traded on secondary markets like:
- **Liv-ex** (London International Vintners Exchange): The professional benchmark index
- **Berry Bros & Rudd**: Major London merchant with investment arm
- **Sotheby's and Christie's**: Major auction houses
- **iDealwine** (France): Online auction platform

Wine appreciates in value as it ages toward its drinking window and becomes scarcer (bottles are opened and drunk). A 2010 Barolo Gran Cru may have cost €50 at release; at peak drinking age (2025–2035), the same bottle may trade at €200–400.

### Risks

- Storage risk (wine that is damaged by heat, light, or poor handling loses value)
- Market risk (prices fluctuate; the fine wine market had a significant correction in 2023–24)
- Fraud risk (counterfeit Bordeaux and Burgundy are an ongoing problem at auction)
- Liquidity risk (you can't quickly sell single bottles at market price)

**For beginners:** Don't buy wine primarily for investment. Buy wine to drink and enjoy. If it also happens to appreciate, that's a bonus. The knowledge required to make consistently profitable wine investment decisions is substantial.

## Formal Wine Education

If this course sparks deeper interest, formal certifications provide structured learning and credentials.

### WSET (Wine & Spirit Education Trust)

The most internationally recognized qualification system.

| Level | Content | Duration |
|-------|---------|---------|
| Level 1 | Basic introduction | 1 day |
| Level 2 | 50 grape varieties, 30+ regions | 2 days + exam |
| Level 3 | Serious depth, tasting exam | Several weeks + exam |
| Level 4 Diploma | Advanced; near-professional level | 2 years |

WSET is available in most countries. Level 2 is the best entry point for a committed enthusiast. Level 3 is where genuine expertise begins.

### Court of Master Sommeliers (CMS)

Prestigious track for hospitality professionals.

| Level | Notes |
|-------|-------|
| Introductory | Entry level, accessible |
| Certified | Serious preparation required |
| Advanced | Very challenging; one of the hardest hospitality exams |
| Master Sommelier | Fewer than 300 exist in the world; legendary difficulty |

### Institute of Masters of Wine (IMW)

The apex academic qualification. Requires thesis, practical tasting, and written exams. Only ~420 Masters of Wine exist globally.

### Informal Learning

No certification required to become knowledgeable. Parallel paths:
- Tasting consistently and keeping notes
- Reading good books (below)
- Visiting regions and producers
- Building a circle of wine-curious friends (tasting groups accelerate learning enormously)

## Recommended Reading

### Foundational Books

- **The World Atlas of Wine** — Hugh Johnson & Jancis Robinson. The indispensable geographic reference. Every wine lover should own it
- **Wine Grapes** — Jancis Robinson, Julia Harding, José Vouillamoz. The definitive 1,300-page encyclopedia of grape varieties. Reference, not light reading
- **How to Drink Wine** — Fiona Beckett. Practical and unpretentious

### Deepening Knowledge

- **Adventures on the Wine Route** — Kermit Lynch. A merchant's journey through France's small producers. One of the best wine books written
- **Making Sense of Burgundy / Making Sense of Champagne / Making Sense of Bordeaux** — Matt Kramer. Each is the best introduction to its subject
- **The Drops of God (Kami no Shizuku)** — manga series by Tadashi Agi. Unusual recommendation but genuinely teaches tasting and regionality through story
- **Orange Wine: An Introduction** — Simon J Woolf. The definitive guide to skin-contact wine, with significant coverage of FVG and Slovenia
- **Amber Revolution** — Simon J Woolf. Same topic, more narrative. Essential for understanding Gravner, Radikon, and the movement they started

### For Slovenia and FVG Specifically

- **Discovering Slovenian Wines** — Various author guides available through Slovenian tourist board
- **Simon Woolf's writing** at *Memorable Experiences of Wine* (blog) — the best English-language coverage of orange and natural wines in this region
- **Slow Wine** (published annually by Slow Food Editore) — the most relevant Italian guide for artisanal and natural producers; strong FVG coverage. Find it by searching "Slow Wine guida Slow Food" — available as a physical book and digitally through Slow Food Italy

---

## Digital Resources

- **Wine-Searcher (wine-searcher.com):** The most useful tool for this region — price research, producer profiles, critic scores. Better coverage of small Slovenian and FVG producers than any rating app. Use before every significant purchase or winery visit
- **Vivino:** Label scanning and quick ratings. Useful for mainstream wines; ratings for small Slovenian/FVG producers are sparse and unreliable — treat with skepticism
- **CellarTracker (cellartracker.com):** Collection management and community tasting notes for mainstream international wines. Coverage of Slovenian producers is very thin
- **Gambero Rosso (gamberorosso.it):** Italy's most authoritative wine guide; searchable by producer. Their Tre Bicchieri award is a reliable quality signal for FVG wines
- **Jancis Robinson (jancisrobinson.com):** Paid subscription but unmatched quality and honesty
- **Decanter (decanter.com):** Good free content; solid FVG and Slovenian coverage in their annual awards
- **WineFolly (winefolly.com):** Visual learning; excellent infographics for beginners
- **Wine Scholar Guild:** Online courses — Italian Wine Scholar is directly relevant

## Self-Quiz

1. What is the difference between organic, biodynamic, and natural wine?
2. Why do some wines benefit from cellaring and others don't?
3. Who were the two pioneers of the orange wine revolution in Oslavia, and what did they change?
4. What is WSET Level 3 and why is it significant for a serious enthusiast?
5. Name two books you'd read to deepen knowledge of FVG and orange wine.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Organic: legally certified; no synthetic chemicals in the vineyard. Biodynamic: organic plus a holistic philosophy (Demeter certified) using lunar calendar, herbal preparations, and treating the farm as a self-sustaining ecosystem. Natural: no single legal definition; a philosophy of minimal intervention in both vineyard and cellar — wild yeast, no or minimal sulfur, no fining/filtration. A wine can be organic without being natural; a natural wine is almost always also organic in the vineyard.

2. Wines that age well have the structural components that allow them to evolve rather than simply deteriorate: high tannin (reds) and/or high acidity (any color), plus the concentration from good terroir and low yields. These elements act as preservatives and building blocks for complexity. Without them, wine oxidizes and loses fruit without gaining anything. Most wine — 90%+ — is made to drink within 1–3 years.

3. Joško Gravner and Stanko Radikon, both from Oslavia in the Collio/Gorizia area. In the 1990s, they rejected modern interventionist winemaking and returned to extended skin contact for white wines, eventually using Georgian clay amphorae (Gravner) and zero sulfur (Radikon). Their wines showed that white wine could have tannin, aging potential, and complexity comparable to red wine — and sparked the global orange wine movement.

4. WSET Level 3 is the third tier of the Wine & Spirit Education Trust qualification. It covers wine in substantial depth — grapes, regions, production, quality assessment, service — and requires both a written exam and a blind tasting exam. It's the level where genuine expertise begins; holders can work in wine trade. For an enthusiast, it represents a structured year of serious learning with real depth.

5. *Amber Revolution* by Simon J. Woolf (narrative history of orange wine, centers on Gravner, Radikon, and the Collio/Brda movement). *Orange Wine: An Introduction* also by Simon J. Woolf (more reference-style, broader coverage). For general depth: *The World Atlas of Wine* (Johnson & Robinson) is essential for every wine lover.

</details>
