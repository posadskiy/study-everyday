# Module 9 — Serving Wine Like a Pro

Serving wine correctly is not snobbery — it's the difference between getting the best out of a bottle and wasting what's inside. Temperature, glassware, and aeration all meaningfully affect what you taste. The investment in getting this right is small.

---

## Temperature

Temperature is the single most impactful serving variable. Too cold suppresses all aromas. Too warm amplifies alcohol and makes reds smell like rubbing alcohol.

### White Wines

| Style | Serving Temperature | Examples |
|-------|-------------------|---------|
| Sparkling (Champagne, Cava, Prosecco) | 6–8°C | Champagne, Crémant, Cava |
| Light, neutral whites | 8–10°C | Pinot Grigio, Muscadet, Vinho Verde |
| Aromatic whites | 8–10°C | Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, Albariño, Zelen, Pinela |
| Medium-bodied whites | 10–12°C | Friulano, Ribolla Gialla, Malvasia Istriana, Vermentino |
| Full-bodied, oaked whites | 12–14°C | White Burgundy (Meursault, Puligny), oaked Chardonnay |
| Sweet whites | 8–10°C | Sauternes, Tokaji, late-harvest Riesling |
| Orange wines | 12–14°C | Gravner, Radikon, skin-contact Rebula — serve slightly warmer to open up aromas |

### Red Wines

| Style | Serving Temperature | Examples |
|-------|-------------------|---------|
| Light reds | 12–14°C | Beaujolais (Gamay), Valpolicella, light Pinot Noir |
| Medium reds | 14–16°C | Burgundy, lighter Sangiovese (Chianti), Barbera |
| Full reds | 16–18°C | Bordeaux, Barolo, Barbaresco, Rhône, Rioja Reserva, Syrah |
| Very full/powerful reds | 17–18°C | Amarone, Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Barossa Shiraz |

**The "room temperature" myth:** "Red wine should be served at room temperature" originated in northern Europe where room temperature was 16–18°C. Modern centrally heated homes in summer are 22–25°C. Serving red wine at that temperature makes it taste hot and alcoholic. **Put your red wine in the fridge for 15–20 minutes before serving.**

### Practical Rules

- **Whites come out of the fridge 10–15 minutes before serving** — too cold is too cold
- **Light reds go in the fridge for 20–30 minutes before serving** — especially Pinot Noir and Beaujolais
- **Full reds need only a brief cooldown** (10–15 minutes in fridge) if your room is above 20°C
- **Champagne stays in ice bucket throughout the meal** — bubbles are more persistent at lower temperature

## Glassware

Wine glasses concentrate aromas at the rim. A good glass makes wine smell more interesting. A bad glass makes even great wine smell of nothing.

### Essential Shapes

**The Universal Wine Glass**
A tulip-shaped glass with a wide bowl tapering to a slightly narrower rim. If you own one type of glass, this is it. Works for both red and white.

**Red Wine Glasses:**

| Glass | Bowl Size | For |
|-------|-----------|-----|
| Bordeaux/Cabernet | Large, tall | Cabernet, Merlot, Syrah, Malbec, Barolo — powerful, tannic reds that need oxygen |
| Burgundy/Pinot | Wide, round bowl (balloon shape) | Pinot Noir, Nebbiolo — delicate wines needing maximum aroma capture |
| Standard red | Medium | General red wine purpose |

**White Wine Glasses:**

| Glass | Bowl Size | For |
|-------|-----------|-----|
| White wine tulip | Medium, narrower | Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, most whites |
| Aromatic white (narrow) | Smaller, more upright | Riesling, Gewürztraminer — focus intense aromas |
| Albariño / light white | Small-medium | Preserve freshness and light aromas |

**For Orange Wine:** Treat like a medium-bodied red wine — a standard Burgundy or universal glass works well. Serve at 12–14°C (warmer than typical white).

**Sparkling Wine:**

- **Flûte:** Traditional, preserves bubbles well, but restricts aromas — acceptable for basic Prosecco/Cava
- **White wine glass (tulip):** Better for Champagne and quality sparkling — allows aromas to develop while still showing bubbles
- **Saucer/coupe:** Pretty at parties, terrible for Champagne — bubbles dissipate in seconds and aromas escape immediately

**Key quality markers in glassware:**
- **Thin rim:** Better than thick-rimmed glasses — the wine flows more naturally
- **Uncolored, clear glass:** See the wine clearly
- **Large enough bowl:** At least 300ml capacity for reds; you fill it only 1/3 full

### Brands Worth Knowing

- **Riedel:** The Austrian company that popularized varietal-specific glasses. Quality ranges from basic (Riedel Ouverture) to premium (Vinum, Veritas, Sommelier series)
- **Zalto:** Thin, elegant Austrian crystal. Very expensive, very fragile, genuinely excellent
- **Spiegelau:** Owned by Riedel, slightly more affordable, excellent quality
- **Schott Zwiesel:** German. Machine-made, more durable than handmade crystal. Good everyday option

You don't need expensive glasses for everyday wine, but a decent set of tulip-shaped glasses (€5–15 each) will noticeably improve your experience over wine tumblers.

## Opening the Bottle

### Corkscrews and Openers

**Sommelier's corkscrew (waiter's friend):** A folding knife with a worm (helix) and lever arm. Standard in restaurants. Requires some practice but is reliable and portable.

**Technique:**
1. Cut the foil below the lip (not the top) using the integrated knife
2. Wipe the top of the cork
3. Insert the worm into the center of the cork at a slight angle, then straighten and drive it in fully
4. Use the notch on the lever to get mechanical advantage and pull the cork out slowly and evenly
5. When the cork is nearly out, angle it to avoid a pop and any wine spilling

**Winged corkscrew:** The common butterfly-shaped opener. Works fine but tends to tear delicate old corks and doesn't work well with synthetic corks.

**Ah-So (two-prong opener):** Two thin metal prongs inserted either side of the cork. Pulls it out without piercing it. Excellent for old, fragile, crumbly corks. A sommelier skill worth learning.

**Coravin:** A device that uses a needle to extract wine through the cork without removing it, replacing extracted wine with argon gas. Allows you to take a glass from a bottle that then stays preserved for weeks/months. Expensive but genuinely useful for single servings from a prized bottle.

### Synthetic Corks and Screwcaps

No shame in screwcaps. New Zealand, Australia, Austria, and Germany increasingly use them. Screwcaps eliminate cork taint (TCA) and reduce oxidation. Many producers have switched entirely.

Synthetic corks are acceptable for everyday wine; for wines intended to age 5+ years, the long-term oxygen exchange properties are less predictable than quality natural cork.

## Decanting

Decanting serves two purposes:

### 1. Removing Sediment (Old Wines)

Wines older than 8–10 years (especially full red wines) develop sediment — polymerized tannin and color compounds that precipitate out of solution. Sediment is harmless but unpleasant to drink.

**How to decant an old wine:**
1. Stand the bottle upright for 24 hours before decanting to let sediment settle to the bottom
2. Light a candle or hold a flashlight under the neck of the bottle
3. Pour slowly and smoothly into the decanter, watching through the neck
4. When you see the cloud of sediment reaching the neck, stop pouring
5. You may lose 2–5 cl of wine — that's fine

### 2. Aeration (Young Tannic Wines)

Young, tannic wines (Barolo, young Bordeaux, Amarone, powerful Syrah) can be harsh and closed when first opened. Oxygen contact:
- Softens tannins
- Opens up aromas that are "closed"
- Reveals the wine's full character

**How to decant a young wine:**
1. Pour vigorously into the decanter — don't be gentle. You want the wine to splash and aerate
2. Let it sit for 30 minutes to 2 hours depending on the wine's power

**Wines that benefit from decanting:**
- Young Barolo/Barbaresco: 1–2 hours
- Young Bordeaux (Cabernet-dominant): 30–60 minutes
- Young Syrah/Hermitage: 30–60 minutes
- Young Rioja Reserva/Gran Reserva: 30–60 minutes
- Amarone: 1–2 hours
- Young Refosco/Pignolo: 30–60 minutes

**Wines that do NOT need decanting:**
- Older fragile Pinot Noir (open and drink within 30 minutes — too much air kills it)
- Delicate aged Burgundy (same reason)
- Most whites
- Beaujolais and light reds
- Old wines that are already declining

**The double decant:** Pour the wine into a decanter, rinse the bottle, pour back. More aeration than a simple pour, useful for very closed young wines.

## Pouring

**How much to pour:**
- Red wine in a large glass: ~125–150ml (the bottom quarter of the bowl) — this leaves room to swirl and concentrates aromas
- White wine in a standard glass: ~125ml
- Champagne: ~100ml into a flûte; can be slightly more in a white wine glass

**Pouring technique:**
- Give the bottle a slight twist as you finish pouring to prevent drips
- Never fill a glass more than halfway
- Pour for guests first, yourself last

## Storage

### Short-Term Storage (1–4 weeks)

- Avoid temperature fluctuation (kitchen near the stove = bad)
- Keep away from direct sunlight (UV light degrades wine)
- If corked, store on its side to keep the cork moist
- Room temperature 16–20°C is fine for short-term

### Once Opened

| Wine Type | Recorked in Fridge | Vacuum-Pumped | Argon Gas |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| Light white | 1–3 days | 3–5 days | 5–7 days |
| Full-bodied white / orange | 3–5 days | 5–7 days | 7+ days |
| Light red | 2–3 days (room temp/fridge) | 3–5 days | 5–7 days |
| Full red | 2–4 days (room temp) | 4–7 days | 7+ days |
| Port / Sherry | Varies: Vintage Port 2 days; Tawny 4 weeks; Fino 1 week refrigerated |

**Argon gas sprays** (Coravin, WineWand, etc.) displace oxygen with inert gas. The most effective method for preservation.

### Long-Term Cellaring

For wines intended to age 5+ years:
- **Constant temperature** 12–14°C (most important factor — fluctuation is damaging)
- **Humidity** 60–80% (keeps corks from drying)
- **Darkness** (UV light degrades wine)
- **No vibration** (debate on this; avoid obviously vibrating environments)
- **Bottles on their sides** (cork contact with wine)

If you don't have a cellar, a wine fridge (a temperature-controlled unit, not a regular food fridge) is the best investment for serious collectors.

## Wine at a Restaurant

### The Tasting Ritual

When the sommelier presents a bottle:
1. **Inspect the label:** Confirm it's what you ordered (vintage, producer)
2. **Smell the cork briefly** after it's presented to you: dampness shows the cork was moist (good storage); excessive mustiness may warn of TCA, but you can't really diagnose cork taint from the cork alone
3. **Wait for the sommelier to pour a small taste**
4. **Swirl and smell:** You're checking for faults (TCA/corked, oxidation, volatile acidity), not approving whether you like the wine
5. **If fine:** "Thank you, that's great" or nod approval
6. **If faulty:** "I think this may be corked" — describe what you smell. A good restaurant will replace it without argument

It is completely acceptable to send back a faulty bottle. It is not acceptable to send back a bottle simply because you don't like the style or because it's different from what you expected.

## Self-Quiz

1. At what temperature should you serve a full-bodied red wine? Why is "room temperature" misleading?
2. What is the purpose of decanting a young Barolo?
3. What glass shape is best for Champagne — flûte or white wine glass, and why?
4. How do you know when to stop pouring when decanting an old wine with sediment?
5. How long will an opened bottle of full-bodied red last if simply recorked?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. 16–18°C. "Room temperature" is misleading because it originates from northern European rooms of the pre-central-heating era (16–18°C). Modern homes in summer are 22–25°C — at that temperature, the alcohol in a full red wine becomes the dominant sensation, making the wine smell and taste "hot." Put your red wine in the fridge for 15 minutes before serving if your room is warm.

2. Aeration. Young Barolo is typically very closed — high tannin, tight structure, not showing its aromatic potential. Pouring it vigorously into a decanter and letting it sit for 1–2 hours allows oxygen contact that softens tannins and opens up the aromas. Without this, many young Barolos taste harsh and austere.

3. A white wine glass (tulip shape) is better. A flûte preserves bubbles well but restricts aromas — you cannot smell the wine properly. A white wine glass allows the aromas to develop and concentrate at the slightly narrower rim, while still showing the bubbles. The flûte is traditional but inferior for appreciating quality Champagne.

4. Hold a candle or flashlight beneath the neck of the bottle as you pour slowly. Watch through the glass for the cloud of sediment moving toward the neck. The moment you see it approaching the opening, stop pouring. You'll lose a small amount of wine — this is unavoidable and correct.

5. 2–4 days at room temperature. After that, oxidation degrades the wine. Using an argon gas spray (which displaces oxygen) extends this to 7+ days. Very tannic, structured reds (Barolo, Amarone) often actually improve on day 2 as the tannins soften — but by day 4–5 the fruit fades.

</details>

## Practical Exercise

**Temperature experiment:** Pour the same red wine into two glasses — one served at refrigerator temperature (~8°C) and one at room temperature (~22°C). Wait 5 minutes, then taste both. The difference in aroma and perceived alcohol demonstrates why temperature matters.
