# Module 8 — Food & Wine Pairing

The goal of food and wine pairing is not to follow rules. It's to understand why certain combinations work — and then use that understanding to make good choices on the fly. You don't need to memorize every classic pairing; you need to understand the principles.

---

## Why Pairing Matters

Food and wine interact chemically. The right pairing:
- Makes the wine taste better
- Makes the food taste better
- Creates harmony where both elements enhance rather than compete

The wrong pairing:
- Makes the wine taste harsh, thin, or off
- Overwhelms the food
- Creates unpleasant reactions (tannin + fish = metallic taste)

---

## The Core Principles

### 1. Match Weight and Intensity

Light food → Light wine  
Rich food → Rich wine

This is the single most important rule. A delicate Dover sole will be obliterated by a Barossa Shiraz. A rich braised short rib will make a Muscadet taste thin and acidic.

**Weight in food:** broth/salad (light) → poached fish (medium-light) → roast chicken (medium) → pork belly (medium-heavy) → braised beef (heavy) → heavily sauced game (very heavy)

**Weight in wine:** Mosel Riesling (very light) → Pinot Grigio (light) → Sancerre (medium-light) → Chardonnay (medium) → Côtes du Rhône rouge (medium-heavy) → Barolo (heavy) → Amarone (very heavy)

### 2. Acidity is Your Friend

High-acid wines work with almost any food. The acidity:
- Cuts through fat and richness (Champagne with fried chicken, Barbera with sausage)
- Refreshes the palate between bites
- Complements sauces and dressed salads

Never pair a low-acid wine (Viognier, Grenache, oaked Chardonnay) with high-acid food — the food will make the wine taste flat.

### 3. Tannin Needs Fat or Protein

Tannins bind to proteins. When they bind to the proteins in your mouth, they create that drying sensation. When they bind to the proteins in a piece of beef, much of that sensation is neutralized — the wine suddenly tastes softer and more fruit-forward.

This is why Cabernet Sauvignon and steak is the classic pairing. The fat and protein in the meat "absorb" some of the tannin.

**Never** pair very tannic red wine (Barolo, Cabernet Sauvignon) with:
- Fish (tannin + fish proteins = metallic, unpleasant)
- High-acid salads (acid amplifies tannin harshness)
- Strongly salted dishes (salt can amplify tannin perception)

### 4. Sweetness Needs to Match or Exceed the Dish

If the food is sweeter than the wine, the wine will taste harsh and acidic by contrast.

- Sweet wine with sweet dessert: works if the wine is at least as sweet as the dessert
- Sauternes with foie gras: works because the wine's sweetness contrasts with the fat and richness
- Dry Riesling with spicy Thai: the food's sugar and spice are moderated by the wine's acidity without a sweetness conflict

### 5. The Mirror Principle: What Grows Together, Goes Together

Regional pairings work because food and wine evolved together in the same place. The locals got it right over centuries.

- Barolo with white truffle pasta (Piedmont)
- Sancerre with goat cheese (Loire Valley)
- Manzanilla Sherry with jamón ibérico (Jerez/Andalucía)
- Muscadet with oysters (Loire Atlantic coast)
- Ribolla Gialla with prosciutto di San Daniele (Friuli)
- Chianti with bistecca fiorentina (Tuscany)
- Chablis with oysters (northern Burgundy near the coast historically)

### 6. The Contrast Principle

Some great pairings work through contrast, not similarity:

- Sweet wine + salty food: Sauternes + Roquefort, Port + Stilton
- Rich wine + light food: Dry Riesling + sushi (the wine's acidity and light body complement the delicate fish)
- High tannin + fat (see above)

### 7. Salt Softens Perception of Tannin and Acidity

Salty food can make a harsh wine taste more approachable. Oysters and Champagne: the saltiness of the oyster softens Champagne's acidity, creating harmony.

## Pairing by Wine Style

### Sparkling Wine
**Most versatile wine category for food pairing.**

- **Champagne / Crémant / Cava (dry, traditional method):** Fried food, shellfish, oysters, sushi, canapés, charcuterie. The acidity and bubbles cut through fat and cleanse the palate
- **Prosecco:** Aperitivo, light appetizers, prosciutto, mild cheeses
- **Moscato d'Asti:** Fresh fruit, light pastries, mild desserts

### Light White Wines (Muscadet, Pinot Grigio, Vinho Verde)
Delicate seafood, light salads, fresh cheeses, oysters, steamed fish, vegetable dishes

### Aromatic Whites (Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, Albariño, Gewürztraminer)
- **Sauvignon Blanc:** Goat cheese, asparagus, green herbs, light seafood, green salads
- **Riesling (dry or off-dry):** Spicy food (Thai, Indian, Sichuan), pork, duck, sushi, smoked salmon
- **Albariño:** Atlantic seafood, octopus, clams, grilled fish
- **Gewürztraminer:** Spicy Asian food, foie gras, blue cheese, washed-rind cheese

### Full-Bodied White Wines (Chardonnay, Viognier, white Burgundy)
Lobster, scallops, cream sauces, roast chicken, veal, grilled fish with butter sauce, pasta with cream

### Orange Wines (skin-contact whites)
These wines have the structure of light reds but the freshness of whites. They pair with:
- Charcuterie, cured meats, pâté
- Roast pork, poultry with herbs
- Aged cheese (especially semi-hard cheeses)
- Middle Eastern and spiced dishes
- Grilled vegetables with olive oil
They're often called "food wines" because the tannin and acidity handle almost anything.

### Light Red Wines (Pinot Noir, Gamay/Beaujolais, lighter Barbera)
Salmon, duck, charcuterie, mushroom dishes, poultry, pasta with meat sauce, mild cheeses

### Medium Red Wines (Merlot, Tempranillo, Sangiovese, Grenache)
Roast chicken, pork, lamb, pasta with meat, pizza, grilled fish (if light on tannin like Grenache), mild game

### Full-Bodied Red Wines (Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Barolo, Malbec, Amarone)
Red meat, lamb, game, hard aged cheeses, braised dishes, rich stews

### Sweet Wines
- **Sauternes / Tokaji:** Foie gras, blue cheese (Roquefort is the classic), fresh fruit desserts
- **Port:** Stilton, walnut cake, chocolate desserts
- **Moscato / Asti:** Fresh fruit, light cakes, not-too-sweet pastry
- **Late-harvest Riesling:** Apple tart, peach desserts, light custard

### Fortified Wines
- **Fino/Manzanilla Sherry:** Jamón, tapas, olives, almonds, seafood — Spain's great aperitif
- **Oloroso Sherry:** Game, offal, roasted nuts, hard cheese
- **Tawny Port:** Hazelnut tart, crème brûlée, dried fruit

### General Pairing Principles for the Region

The cuisines of Friuli and Slovenia are **hearty, fatty, and earthy** — brovada, jota, pršut, frico. They need wines with:
- **High acidity** to cut through fat
- **Earthy character** to match the food's character
- **Some tannin** for the meat dishes

This is exactly what Refosco, Teran, Schioppettino, and orange wines provide. The regional pairing is not accidental.

## Common Pairing Mistakes

1. **Tannic red with fish:** The interaction produces a metallic, unpleasant taste. A light, low-tannin red (Gamay, Grenache) can work with heartier fish, but Cabernet with sole is a mistake

2. **Sweet food with dry wine:** Chocolate cake with a dry Cabernet makes the wine taste harsh and sour. Needs a sweet wine (Port, PX Sherry, late-harvest Zinfandel)

3. **Very oaky wine with delicate food:** A heavily oaked California Chardonnay will overpower grilled sole. Use unoaked or lightly oaked white instead

4. **Ordering wine before seeing the menu:** The wine should match the food, not the other way around

5. **High acid food (vinaigrette, lemon) with low acid wine:** Makes the wine taste flat. Match acidity levels

6. **Very salty food with high-tannin wine:** Salt amplifies tannin harshness

## Self-Quiz

1. Why does steak pair so well with Cabernet Sauvignon?
2. What wine style is most versatile for food pairing, and why?
3. Why shouldn't you pair a very dry wine with a sweet dessert?
4. What is the "regional pairing" principle and give a local example?
5. What traditional Friulian or Slovenian dish would you pair with Refosco and why?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Cabernet Sauvignon has high tannin. Tannins bind to proteins and fats. The fat and protein in red meat absorb much of the tannin, making the wine taste softer and more fruit-forward, while the fat's richness is cut through by the wine's acidity. Both are improved by the interaction.

2. Dry sparkling wine (Champagne, Cava, Crémant). High acidity + bubbles cut through fat and refresh the palate with almost any food. Works with fried food, shellfish, charcuterie, salty snacks, and light appetizers. The only things it doesn't handle well are very sweet desserts and very heavy red meat.

3. When food is sweeter than the wine, the wine tastes harsh and sour by contrast — its acidity becomes aggressive. You need the wine to be at least as sweet as the dessert, which is why Port goes with Stilton and Sauternes goes with foie gras.

4. Foods and wines that evolved together in the same region naturally complement each other — centuries of local cooking and viticulture developed in harmony. Local example: kraški pršut (dry-cured Karst ham, very salty and savory) with Vitovska (mineral, saline, high acidity that cuts through the fat) or Teran (dense, tannic — the tannin binds to the meat's protein).

5. Brovada e muset — turnips fermented in grape marc, served with a boiled sausage. The dish is earthy, fatty, and tangy. Refosco's high tannin handles the fat and protein, its high acidity matches the fermented sourness of the turnips, and its dark earthy character mirrors the dish's flavors. The regional principle in full effect.

</details>
