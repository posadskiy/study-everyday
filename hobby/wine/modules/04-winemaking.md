# Module 4 — How Wine is Made

You don't need to understand every detail of winemaking to appreciate wine. But understanding the major decisions a winemaker makes — and why — changes how you taste. Each choice in the cellar leaves a fingerprint in the glass.

---

## From Vine to Bottle: The Overview

```
Harvest → Sorting → Crushing/Destemming → Fermentation → Aging → Blending → Fining/Filtration → Bottling
```

Each step has variables. The differences between a cheap supermarket wine and a Grand Cru Burgundy are determined by decisions at every stage.

---

## 1. Growing Season and Harvest

Winemaking begins in the vineyard. The winemaker (or their team) monitors sugar levels (*Brix* or *Öchsle*), acidity, and phenolic ripeness throughout the growing season.

**Harvest timing** is one of the most critical decisions:
- **Early harvest:** Higher acidity, lower alcohol, fresher, lighter wine — the style for Champagne and fine German Riesling
- **Late harvest:** Higher sugar (more alcohol or sweeter wine), riper fruit flavors, lower acidity
- **Machine harvest vs. hand harvest:** Machines are faster and cheaper but less selective. Hand harvesting allows sorting of rotten or unripe bunches in the vineyard. Prestige estates harvest by hand

**Yield** matters. Fewer grapes per vine = more concentration in each grape. High-yield vineyards produce dilute wine. The EU and most appellation rules legally limit yields for quality reasons.

### Vineyard Location and Exposure

The character of a wine is shaped by decisions made long before harvest — often before the vine is even planted.

**Slope orientation** in the Northern Hemisphere:
- South-facing slopes receive the most sunlight → warmer, riper grapes; preferred in cool regions
- North-facing slopes stay cooler → higher acidity, less ripeness; sometimes chosen in hot regions to preserve freshness
- East-facing slopes get gentler morning sun; west-facing get hotter afternoon sun

**Altitude** provides a natural way to dial down temperature. Higher vineyards are cooler, which slows ripening and preserves acidity — this is why Malbec from the high-altitude Uco Valley in Mendoza has more freshness than from the valley floor.

**Diurnal temperature variation** (the difference between day and night temperatures) is one of the most important factors for aroma and freshness. Hot days ripen sugars; cold nights slow the process and preserve the volatile aromatic compounds in the grape. Regions with large swings (Friuli, Alsace, Napa Mountain, Mendoza) tend to produce aromatic, fresh wines despite warm growing seasons.

### Seasonal Vineyard Work

Premium wine requires active management throughout the growing season. The vine's natural tendency is to overproduce — more shoots, more leaves, more grapes. Left unmanaged, yield increases but quality drops.

- **Winter pruning:** Limits the number of buds that will become shoots, restricting yield and concentrating flavor in the remaining fruit
- **Shoot thinning:** Extra green shoots are removed in spring — improves airflow, reduces fungal disease risk, forces the vine to direct energy into fewer clusters
- **Leaf removal:** Leaves around the grape clusters are partially removed to improve sun exposure and ventilation. Too much removal in a hot season can sunburn the grapes
- **Green harvest (*vendemmia verde*):** Some green grape clusters are cut off and discarded in summer — the remaining clusters ripen more fully with concentrated flavor. Common in premium DOC/DOCG production. Painful for producers who throw away potential income; the payoff is in the glass

### Assessing Ripeness: More Than a Refractometer

Sugar and acidity are measured instrumentally (refractometer for Brix, pH meter for acidity). But phenolic ripeness — the quality of tannins in the skins and seeds — cannot be measured by a machine. Winemakers assess it by tasting the grapes directly:

- **Skins:** Unripe skins taste bitter, vegetal, and harsh; ripe skins are softer, fruitier, and less aggressive
- **Seeds:** Green seeds contribute harsh, bitter tannins to the wine; brown seeds indicate phenolic ripeness
- **Juice:** Tasted for flavour and acid balance

This is why harvest dates are set by walking the vineyard and tasting, not by reading a spreadsheet.

## 2. Sorting and Crushing

After harvest, grapes arrive at the winery. Quality producers sort at the sorting table (*table de tri*) — removing leaves, unripe or rotten grapes, insects.

**Destemming:** Removing the grape stems before fermentation. Stems contain green, bitter tannins. Most winemakers destem fully. However, some Burgundian producers use *whole cluster fermentation* (keeping stems) for added structure and complexity.

**Crushing:** Breaking the grape skins to release juice. Gentler is better — very aggressive crushing releases bitter compounds from seeds. Top estates use minimal crushing and let gravity do the work.

**White vs. Red:** The first major fork in the road.
- **White wine:** Grapes are pressed immediately to separate juice from skins before fermentation
- **Red wine:** Skins stay in contact with the juice during fermentation (this extracts color, tannin, and flavor)
- **Rosé:** Either brief skin contact with red grapes, or blending red and white wine (rare in quality production)

## 3. Fermentation

**Alcoholic fermentation:** Yeast converts grape sugars into alcohol + CO₂. The reaction is exothermic (produces heat) and must be temperature-controlled.

```
Sugars + Yeast → Ethanol (alcohol) + CO₂
```

**Temperature:** Lower temperatures (12–15°C) preserve fresh, fruity aromas in white wines. Higher temperatures (25–30°C) extract more color and tannin in reds. Temperature control is one of the most important advances of 20th-century winemaking.

**Yeast choices:**
- **Commercial yeasts:** Reliable, predictable, selected strains. Give consistent results. Used widely in commercial winemaking
- **Wild/indigenous yeasts:** Naturally present on grape skins and in the cellar. Unpredictable but produce more complex, site-specific flavors. Used by natural wine producers and quality estates. Slower fermentation with higher risk

**Yeast does far more than convert sugar to alcohol.** During fermentation, yeast metabolism produces a wide range of aromatic compounds — esters, thiols, and higher alcohols — that become a significant part of the finished wine's aroma. Some of these compounds barely exist in the grape at all; they are created entirely by yeast during fermentation.

Examples of fermentation-created aromas:
- *Isoamyl acetate* (an ester) → banana, bubblegum, pear candy — prominent in carbonic maceration wines (Beaujolais) and some Prosecco
- *Rotundone* (a sesquiterpene) → black pepper — found naturally in Syrah but amplified by certain yeasts and fermentation conditions
- *Thiols* → tropical fruit (passionfruit, guava) — precursors are present in the grape but released and transformed by yeast enzymes; critical to the tropical character of New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc

Different yeast strains can emphasize or suppress different aromatic families in the same grape. A Sauvignon Blanc fermented with one strain may lean green and mineral (methoxypyrazines dominant); with another strain it may lean tropical and floral (thiols dominant). This is one reason two wines made from the same grapes can smell very different.

However, yeast works within the aromatic boundaries set by the grape variety. It can amplify, suppress, or reshape balance, but it cannot transform a Sauvignon Blanc into a Riesling. The variety defines what aromatic potential exists; yeast determines which parts of that potential are expressed.

**Fermentation vessels:**
- **Stainless steel tanks:** Clean, neutral, temperature-controllable. Preserves fresh fruit character. The dominant choice for aromatic whites (Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, Pinot Grigio)
- **Oak barrels:** Adds texture, complexity, vanilla/spice notes, allows micro-oxygenation (small oxygen contact through the wood). Used for wines intended to age
- **Concrete tanks (especially eggs):** Neutral like steel but with slight mineral exchange. Some producers prefer for certain whites
- **Clay amphorae:** Ancient technology revived in FVG and Georgia. Allows slow oxygenation without oak flavor. Central to the orange wine style

## 4. Maceration (Reds and Orange Wines)

For red wines, **maceration** is the process of grape skins soaking in the fermenting juice. This extracts:
- **Color** (anthocyanins from the skins)
- **Tannin** (structure, aging potential)
- **Flavor compounds**

**Techniques to increase extraction:**
- *Punch-down (pigeage)*: Manually pushing the floating "cap" of skins back into the liquid
- *Pump-over (remontage)*: Pumping juice from the bottom of the tank over the cap
- *Délestage (rack and return)*: Draining the juice, leaving the cap to drain, then returning the juice

**Maceration length** determines the wine's power:
- Short maceration (few days): Light reds, early-drinking, like Beaujolais or some Dolcetto
- Long maceration (2–4 weeks+): Powerful, tannic reds for aging — Barolo, serious Cabernet Sauvignon

### Why Tannins Matter for Aging

Tannins do more than create grip in young wine — they are the structural backbone that makes long aging possible.

Chemically, tannins are polyphenols. In wine, they act as an oxidative buffer: they absorb oxygen that enters the bottle over time, which would otherwise degrade the fruit compounds. In effect, the tannins take the hit so the wine doesn't.

Over decades, tannin molecules also **polymerize** — they link together into longer chains, which are perceived as softer and silkier on the palate. This is the physical mechanism behind "tannins softening with age." Simultaneously, tannins bind with anthocyanins (color pigments), forming stable complexes that drop out as sediment in old bottles and explain why the wine's color gradually fades from deep ruby to brick and garnet at the rim.

This is why high-tannin grapes (Nebbiolo, Cabernet Sauvignon, Schioppettino, Pignolo) are the best candidates for long cellaring, and why low-tannin wines (Pinot Grigio, Grenache, Gamay) are generally made to be drunk young.

### Orange Wine (Skin-Contact White Wine)

This is a white wine made with extended skin contact — the same way red wine is made.

**How it works:** White grapes are not pressed immediately. Instead, the skins are left in contact with the juice for days, weeks, or even months during fermentation. The skins extract tannin, color (turning the wine from pale yellow to amber/orange), and complex flavor compounds.

**Results:** The wine has structure and tannin unusual in white wines, oxidative notes, and a very different aroma profile — dried fruit, nuts, tea, spice, quince.

**Why it matters for FVG and Slovenia:** The orange wine revolution started here. Joško Gravner of Oslavia (FVG) began experimenting with long skin-contact and Georgian clay amphorae in the 1990s, influenced by a trip to Georgia. Stanko Radikon (also Oslavia) followed. This approach is now closely associated with the Collio/Brda border zone.

**Not the same as orange-flavored wine.** The name comes from the color, not any flavor additive.

## 5. Malolactic Fermentation (MLF)

**What it is:** A secondary bacterial conversion that transforms sharp malic acid (found in green apples) into softer lactic acid (found in milk).

```
Malic acid (sharp) → Lactic acid (soft) + CO₂
```

**Effect on wine:**
- Reduces perceived acidity
- Adds buttery, creamy texture (diacetyl)
- Stabilizes the wine against spoilage in bottle

**Who uses it:**
- Almost all red wines undergo full MLF
- Full-bodied Chardonnay (Burgundy, California) undergoes MLF — it's what creates the butter note
- Aromatic whites (Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling) often have MLF blocked to preserve freshness and acidity

## 6. Oak Aging

Oak is one of the most significant flavor contributors in wine. It adds:
- **Vanilla** (vanillin from oak)
- **Toast, smoke, spice** (from barrel charring/toasting)
- **Cedar, pencil shavings** (French oak characteristic)
- **Coconut, dill** (American oak characteristic)
- **Micro-oxygenation:** Tiny amounts of oxygen pass through the wood, softening tannins and adding complexity

**Barrel types:**
- **French oak** (*chêne français*): Subtler, more fine-grained tannin and flavor. More expensive. Used in Burgundy, Bordeaux, fine Italian wines
- **American oak** (*chêne américain*): Stronger flavor (more vanilla, coconut). Used in traditional Rioja, Australian Shiraz
- **New vs. used barrels:** New barrels impart the most flavor. With each use, the oak impact diminishes. Many producers use a mix of new and 1–3 year-old barrels

**Barrel size:**
- **Barrique** (225L, Bordeaux standard): Smaller = more oak surface area per liter of wine = more impact
- **Burgundy pièce** (228L): Similar
- **Tonneaux** (500L): Less oak impact
- **Large foudres/botti** (1000–10,000L): Used in Barolo, Chianti — minimal oak flavor, mostly used for gentle oxidation and stabilization

**Alternative oak contact:**
- **Oak staves/chips/powder:** Cheaper alternative to barrels. Gives oak flavor without the micro-oxygenation benefits. Legal in most countries (except France and Italy for fine wines). Common in commercial wines

**No oak:** Many high-quality whites are made entirely in stainless steel to preserve pure fruit and acidity. Classic for Chablis, Muscadet, Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio.

## 7. Blending

Most wine is a blend. The Bordeaux blend (Cabernet Sauvignon + Merlot + Cabernet Franc + Petit Verdot + Malbec) is the most famous. Châteauneuf-du-Pape permits 13 varieties.

Even single-varietal wines often blend different barrels, vineyard blocks, or vintages to achieve consistency and complexity.

**Non-vintage (NV)** wines (most Champagne, Fino Sherry, basic Ports) are intentionally blended across multiple vintages to maintain a consistent house style.

---

## 8. Fining and Filtration

Wine stabilization prevents haze, crystal deposits, re-fermentation in bottle, and microbial spoilage. The main methods:

**Cold stabilization:** The wine is chilled close to freezing for 1–2 weeks. Tartaric acid (naturally present in grapes) crystallizes and precipitates out. Without this step, bottles can develop harmless white crystals after refrigeration — they look alarming but are completely neutral in taste. Many quality producers skip cold stabilization, letting the crystals form naturally.

**Fining:** Adding a substance that binds to particles in the wine and makes them drop out. Common agents: bentonite clay (used for protein stability in whites — prevents haze caused by heat-unstable proteins), egg whites, isinglass (fish-derived), casein (milk protein), activated charcoal. *Vegan wines* use bentonite or nothing.

**Filtration:** Passing wine through filters to remove yeast cells, bacteria, and particles. Improves clarity and stability.

**Natural wine perspective:** Many natural wine producers skip or minimize fining and filtration entirely, arguing they remove character from the wine. This can leave the wine slightly cloudy ("voile") and potentially less stable after opening.

## 9. Sparkling Wine Methods

### Traditional Method (Méthode Traditionnelle / Champenoise)

The best sparkling wine method. Second fermentation in the bottle.

```
Base wine → Add yeast + sugar (liqueur de tirage) → Bottle → 
Second fermentation in bottle → Aging on lees (autolysis adds biscuit/brioche) → 
Riddling (remuage) → Disgorgement → Dosage → Final cork
```

**Results:** Persistent, fine bubbles. Toasty, biscuit, complex secondary aromas from lees contact. Used for Champagne, Crémant (France), Cava (Spain), Franciacorta (Italy), traditional Sekt (Germany), Pétillant Naturel (pet-nat).

**Why a bottle and not a barrel:** The second fermentation produces CO₂, which must remain dissolved in the wine under pressure. A barrel is porous — CO₂ escapes through the wood and pressure cannot build. A sealed glass bottle is the only container that holds pressure safely while the process completes. This is also why traditional method sparkling wine bottles are thick-walled (about twice the weight of a still wine bottle) and use a wire cage over the cork.

**Lees contact time:** Non-vintage Champagne: minimum 15 months. Vintage: minimum 3 years. Prestige cuvées (Dom Pérignon, Krug): often 6–10 years.

### Charmat Method (Tank Method / Metodo Italiano)

Second fermentation in a pressurized tank, not in bottle.

- Faster and cheaper than traditional method
- Preserves fresh, fruity, floral aromas (better for aromatic grapes)
- Used for: Prosecco, Moscato d'Asti, Lambrusco, most commercial sparkling wines

### Pétillant Naturel (Pét-Nat)

Ancient "ancestral method." Wine is bottled before primary fermentation is complete. The remaining sugars ferment in bottle, creating natural carbonation. No added yeast or sugar.

- Lightly sparkling, often cloudy (no disgorgement)
- Can be unpredictable
- Very fashionable in natural wine circles
- Common in Loire, FVG, Slovenia

## 10. Fortified Wines

Fortified wines have spirits (usually neutral grape brandy) added during or after fermentation.

| Wine | Origin | Style | How Made |
|------|--------|-------|----------|
| Port | Douro Valley, Portugal | Sweet red | Brandy added during fermentation (stops it, retaining sugar) |
| Sherry | Jerez, Spain | Dry to sweet | Brandy added after fermentation; aged in solera system |
| Madeira | Madeira, Portugal | Dry to sweet | Heated during aging (estufagem) — the only intentionally "cooked" fine wine |
| Marsala | Sicily, Italy | Dry to sweet | Brandy added; various aging systems |

## How Winemaking Choices Affect What You Taste

| Winemaking Choice | Effect on Wine |
|-------------------|----------------|
| Early harvest | Higher acidity, lower alcohol, lighter body |
| Late harvest | Lower acidity, higher alcohol, riper fruit |
| Stainless steel | Crisp, pure fruit, no oak flavor |
| New French oak | Vanilla, cedar, increased structure |
| American oak | Vanilla, coconut, more obvious |
| Long maceration | High tannin, deep color, structure for aging |
| Short maceration | Light color, low tannin, drink early |
| MLF completed | Soft, buttery, round acidity |
| MLF blocked | Sharp, fresh, crisp acidity |
| Skin contact (white) | Tannin, amber color, oxidative complexity |
| Wild yeast | More complexity and variability |

## Self-Quiz

1. What is the key difference in how red and white wines are made after crushing?
2. What is malolactic fermentation and what does it do to a wine's taste?
3. What is the difference between the traditional method and Charmat method for sparkling wine?
4. Why does Barolo typically use large oak barrels (*botti*) rather than small barriques?
5. What makes orange wine "orange" — is it the grapes or the production method?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. White wine grapes are pressed immediately to separate the juice from the skins before fermentation. Red wine grapes ferment with the skins still in contact, which extracts the color, tannin, and flavor compounds from the skins.

2. A bacterial process that converts sharp malic acid (green apple) into softer lactic acid (milk). The result: reduced perceived acidity, a creamier or buttery texture, and a more rounded mouthfeel. Almost all reds undergo it; it's blocked in many aromatic whites to preserve freshness.

3. Traditional method: the second fermentation happens inside individual bottles, the wine ages on the lees in bottle, producing fine persistent bubbles and bready/toasty complexity. Used for Champagne, Cava, Franciacorta. Charmat method: the second fermentation happens in a pressurized tank, then the wine is bottled. Faster, cheaper, preserves fresh fruity aromas. Used for Prosecco.

4. Large barrels (up to 10,000L) have a very small surface area of oak relative to the volume of wine, so they impart minimal oak flavor. They are used primarily for gradual, gentle oxygenation and stability — not to add vanilla/toast character. Barolo's traditional style aims to showcase Nebbiolo's fruit and tannin, not cover it with oak.

5. The production method. Orange wine is white wine made with extended skin contact (days to months), exactly like red wine. The skins give the wine its amber/orange color, tannin, and complex flavors. The grapes themselves are regular white-wine grapes; the color comes entirely from the technique.

</details>
