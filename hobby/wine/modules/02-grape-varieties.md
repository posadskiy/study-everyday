# Module 2 — Grape Varieties

There are thousands of wine grape varieties (*Vitis vinifera*). You don't need to know them all. This module covers three tiers:

1. **FVG & Slovenian native grapes** — these are what you will actually drink and taste week to week. Learn these first.
2. **Major Italian grapes** — the broader Italian context you will encounter in local shops and restaurants.
3. **International reference grapes** — available everywhere, useful for building comparison points and understanding the global wine vocabulary.

Every grape entry follows the same structure:
> **History & name · Style · Taste · Aromas · Aging · Classic regions · Food**

---

## Part 1: FVG & Slovenian Native Grapes

These are the varieties unique to — or best expressed in — your local region. Start here. You can buy and taste most of these for under €15.

---

### Friulano (Tocai Friulano / Jakot)

**History & name**
The grape has been documented in Friuli since at least the 17th century under the name Tocai Friulano, likely derived from a village name rather than any connection to Hungary. In 2007 the EU banned the name "Tocai" to prevent confusion with Hungarian Tokaji. Italy renamed it *Friulano*. Slovenia took a more pointed approach: the Slovenian name became *Jakot* — Tocai spelled backwards. The renaming was considered an insult to a grape with no historical link to Hungary, but the EU ruling stood.

**Style**
Medium body. Medium-high acidity. Dry. Low alcohol (typically 12–13%). No tannin (white wine).

**Taste**
Clean and precise on the palate. Light-to-medium weight. A pronounced bitter almond note on the finish is the most recognizable characteristic — almost no other grape does this. Lingering, dry finish. Some mineral quality in top examples from ponca soils.

**Aromas**
Hay, white peach, almond, mild citrus, fennel. In aged examples a slight nuttiness develops. Subtle rather than expressive — not a perfumed grape.

**Aging**
Best at 1–4 years from vintage. Top single-vineyard examples (Miani, Borgo del Tiglio) can hold 8–10 years, gaining complexity without losing freshness.

**Classic regions**
Collio DOC, Colli Orientali del Friuli (COF) DOC, Friuli Isonzo DOC (Italy). Goriška Brda ZGP (Slovenia, as Jakot).

**Food**
Prosciutto di San Daniele, frico (fried Montasio cheese with potato), mild salumi, frittata, fresh seafood, light pasta with cream or butter. The bitter finish works particularly well with fatty, cured meat.

### Ribolla Gialla / Rebula

**History & name**
One of the oldest documented grapes in northeastern Italy. First recorded in Gorizia in 1299. *Ribolla* derives from the Latin *rebolire* — "to boil again" — possibly referring to re-fermentation in the barrel. In Slovenian it is *Rebula*, a phonetic adaptation. The same grape, the same hillside, two names from the border that cut through both in 1947.

**Style**
Conventional: medium body, high acidity, bone dry, light-to-medium alcohol.
Skin-contact (orange wine): medium-full body, significant tannin (unusual for a white), amber color, still dry.

**Taste**
Conventional: lively and fresh, citrus-led with a clean, slightly tart finish. Very food-friendly.
Orange wine style: much more complex — texture similar to a light red, long finish with dried fruit, walnut, and tannic grip. Can be austere when young; opens beautifully with age.

**Aromas**
Conventional: green apple, white peach, lemon zest, fresh citrus.
Orange wine: dried apricot, walnut skin, chamomile tea, ginger, beeswax, dried orange peel.

**Aging**
Conventional: 2–5 years.
Orange wine (Gravner, Radikon, Movia Lunar): released 5–7 years after harvest; best at 10–20+ years. Some bottles are still evolving at 25 years.

**Classic regions**
Collio DOC, especially Oslavia (Italy). Goriška Brda ZGP (Slovenia). The grape exists elsewhere in small quantities but reaches its peak on these ponca hills.

**Food**
Conventional: risotto, seafood pasta, grilled white fish, light cheeses.
Orange wine: roast pork, aged semi-hard cheese, charcuterie, grilled vegetables, game birds. Treat it like a light red.

### Malvasia Istriana

**History & name**
*Malvasia* is not a single grape but a large family of loosely related varieties spread from Greece through the Mediterranean. The name derives from *Monemvasia*, a Greek port from which these grapes were historically shipped. Malvasia Istriana is the specific variety of the Istrian peninsula and Friulian coast — distinct from Malvasia di Lipari (Sicily), Malmsey (Madeira), or Malvasia di Candia. It arrived on the Adriatic coast centuries ago through Venetian trade routes.

**Style**
Medium body. Medium acidity (lower than Friulano or Ribolla). Dry to slightly off-dry. Low-medium alcohol.

**Taste**
Soft and slightly round on the palate. Gentle bitterness on the finish (similar to Friulano but milder). Some examples have a very slight residual sweetness that rounds the mouthfeel without being detectable as sweet.

**Aromas**
Apricot, white peach, orange blossom, almond, a hint of bitter herbs. More perfumed than Friulano but less intensely aromatic than Gewürztraminer. Elegant rather than showy.

**Aging**
Drink within 2–4 years. Not a wine for cellaring.

**Classic regions**
Carso DOC (Italy), Collio DOC (Italy), Goriška Brda ZGP (Slovenia), Istria (Croatia). Also found widely in Slovenian Istra as the primary local white.

**Food**
Grilled or baked seafood, risotto di mare, smoked fish, bruschetta, mild fresh cheeses. Its soft roundness suits delicate seafood that Ribolla's acidity might overpower.

### Vitovska

**History & name**
An ancient variety believed to be indigenous to the Carso/Kras plateau. The name is of Slovenian origin; exact etymology is disputed but most likely derives from a village name in the Kras area. Virtually unknown outside its home territory — finding it outside the triangle of Trieste, Gorizia, and the Karst plateau is unusual. First formally classified in the 20th century, though locals had grown it for centuries.

**Style**
Medium body. High acidity. Bone dry. Low-medium alcohol. Pronounced mineral and saline quality that sets it apart from any other white grape in this module.

**Taste**
Stony, almost chalky texture on the palate. Saline finish that reminds some tasters of oyster liquid or sea air. High acidity keeps it very fresh and taut. Lean — not a rich wine.

**Aromas**
White flowers, citrus peel, flint, chalk, green herbs, a distinctive mineral-saline note that is almost impossible to confuse with other grapes.

**Aging**
2–6 years from vintage. Top examples from Edi Kante or Zidarich can hold 8+ years.

**Classic regions**
Carso DOC (Italy), Kras ZGP (Slovenia). Grown almost nowhere else on earth.

**Food**
Oysters, raw shellfish, grilled fish, raw fish preparations, Karst cheese (*kraški sir*), cold cuts from the Karst. The salinity in the wine mirrors the salinity in the seafood — a near-perfect regional pairing.

### Pinela

**History & name**
One of Slovenia's most ancient indigenous varieties, documented in the Vipava Valley since the 18th century. The name is of obscure local origin — possibly derived from a Slavic root related to *pine* or *meadow*, though this is uncertain. It grows almost exclusively in the Vipava Valley and is protected as a local heritage variety. Production is tiny; most bottles never leave Slovenia.

**Style**
Light-to-medium body. High acidity. Bone dry. Low alcohol (often 11–12%). Delicate in every sense.

**Taste**
Light and fresh on the palate. Crisp acidity, very clean finish. Mild flavors — not a wine that asserts itself. Its appeal is in subtlety and the sense of place it carries from the Vipava Valley.

**Aromas**
White flowers, fresh lemon, green apple, chamomile, a faint herbal note. Gentle and restrained.

**Aging**
Drink within 1–3 years. Not intended for cellaring.

**Classic regions**
Vipavska Dolina ZGP (Vipava Valley), Slovenia. Essentially nowhere else.

**Food**
Light salads, aperitivo, fresh goat cheese, poultry, vegetable dishes. A spring and summer wine.

---

### Zelen

**History & name**
*Zelen* means "green" in Slovenian — the name refers to the grape's fresh herbal character and the green colour it retains even at full ripeness. Like Pinela, it is indigenous to the Vipava Valley and has been documented there for centuries. It is a protected heritage variety under Slovenian wine law. Together with Pinela it defines the Vipava Valley's unique white wine identity. Very rarely found outside Slovenia.

**Style**
Light body. High acidity. Bone dry. Low alcohol. More herbaceous in character than Pinela.

**Taste**
Crisp and refreshing. Herbal quality runs through the palate — green pepper, fresh herbs, a slight grassy note. Clean, short-to-medium finish. Very easy to drink.

**Aromas**
Freshly cut herbs, nettle, green apple, lemon, white flowers. Distinctly green and herbaceous — more expressive than Pinela on the nose.

**Aging**
Drink within 1–3 years from vintage.

**Classic regions**
Vipavska Dolina ZGP (Vipava Valley), Slovenia. Protected indigenous variety.

**Food**
Grilled fish, vegetable pasta, herb-heavy dishes, risotto with herbs or asparagus, aperitivo. The herbal quality works well with food that contains similar flavors.

---

### Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso / Teran

**History & name**
Refosco is one of the oldest cultivated grapes in northeastern Italy, with documentation stretching back to the Roman era. The full name *Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso* (Refosco with the red stalk) distinguishes this variety from other Refosco biotypes by its characteristic red petiole. *Teran* is the name used on the Slovenian Kras for what is widely considered a local biotype of the same grape, though its exact genetic relationship is debated. Slovenia fought and won an EU legal dispute to protect the name Teran as a PDO exclusively for wines from the Kras region.

**Style**
Medium-full body. High tannin. High acidity. Medium alcohol. Refosco is slightly lighter; Teran is notably denser, almost inky purple-black, with exceptionally high polyphenol content.

**Taste**
Refosco: dark cherry, bitter herbs on the palate, tannic grip, dry finish with some earthiness.
Teran: more intense — almost purple in flavor, very tannic and acidic, with a distinctive iron-like or blood-orange quality that comes from the Kras limestone soils. Demanding wine; it needs fatty food.

**Aromas**
Dark cherry, black olive, dried plum, earth, bitter herbs, tobacco. Teran adds an iron-mineral note and a depth of color rarely seen in other grapes.

**Aging**
3–8 years for quality examples. Teran from great vintages can hold 10+ years.

**Classic regions**
Colli Orientali del Friuli DOC, Friuli Aquileia DOC (Italy). Kras ZGP (Slovenia, as Teran PDO).

**Food**
Brovada e muset (fermented turnips with sausage — the classic Friulian pairing), game, braised meats, aged hard cheese. Teran is traditionally paired with kraški pršut on the Kras — the tannin and the fat of the ham find equilibrium.

### Schioppettino

**History & name**
*Schioppettino* means "little gun" or "crackle" in Friulian dialect — the name refers to the popping of the grape skin under pressure, or alternatively to the wine's peppery character that "crackles" in the mouth. Documented in the Prepotto area of COF since the 13th century. By the 1970s it had nearly vanished — commercial pressures pushed producers toward international varieties, and it was de-listed from the DOC. A group of local producers, particularly the Rapuzzi family at Ronchi di Cialla, campaigned for years to have it legally reinstated. They succeeded in 1978. It is now one of COF's most celebrated indigenous reds.

**Style**
Medium body. Firm but fine-grained tannin. High acidity. Medium alcohol.

**Taste**
Wild berry fruit on the palate, then a pronounced black pepper hit — one of the clearest expressions of peppery spice in any red grape. Dry finish with lingering spice and herbs. The tannins are present but never coarse.

**Aromas**
Wild blackberry, black pepper (very pronounced), violet, wild herbs, sometimes a smoky or earthy note. The pepper is the defining aroma — more intense than in Syrah and more wild in character.

**Aging**
5–12 years. Young Schioppettino is good but the pepper integrates beautifully with age. The best examples open up magnificently at 8–10 years.

**Classic regions**
Colli Orientali del Friuli DOC, especially the sub-zone of Prepotto (Italy). Very little planted elsewhere.

**Food**
Game birds (pheasant, quail), venison, mushroom dishes, aged local cheese. The pepper in the wine mirrors the pepper used to season game in Friulian cooking.

---

### Pignolo

**History & name**
*Pignolo* means "pine cone" in Italian — the grape cluster is tight and compact like a pine cone, which makes it difficult to grow (compact clusters are vulnerable to rot). Documented in the Rosazzo area of COF since the 15th century. Like Schioppettino, it almost disappeared in the 20th century and was saved by dedicated local producers, particularly Roberto Scubla and the Abbazia di Rosazzo. Production remains tiny; it is one of the rarest wines in FVG.

**Style**
Full body. Very high tannin. High acidity. Medium-high alcohol. One of the most structured wines in FVG — demanding significant aging.

**Taste**
Dense and concentrated on the palate. Layers of dark fruit, earth, leather. Very long finish. Young Pignolo can be almost unapprovachable — the tannins are formidable. With 8–10 years the wine opens into something complex and remarkable.

**Aromas**
Black cherry, dried plum, leather, dark earth, dark chocolate, dried herbs. Complex and austere rather than immediately fruity.

**Aging**
Minimum 5–8 years. Best at 10–15 years. Some exceptional examples are worth holding 20 years.

**Classic regions**
Colli Orientali del Friuli DOC (Italy). Tiny production — expect to find it mainly at the winery or specialist enoteca.

**Food**
Braised wild boar, venison stew, game birds, aged hard cheese. Not a casual dinner wine — treat it like Barolo: plan the meal around it.

---

### Tazzelenghe

**History & name**
*Tazzelenghe* means "tongue-cutter" in Friulian dialect — a direct reference to the wine's formidable acidity, which was said to cut the tongue of those who drank it. The grape is indigenous to the Colli Orientali area and has been grown there for centuries, though like many Friulian indigenous reds it declined severely in the 20th century. Production today is very small; it is less well-known internationally than Schioppettino or Pignolo.

**Style**
Medium body. High tannin. Very high acidity — the defining characteristic. Medium alcohol.

**Taste**
Sharp, tart, and intensely acidic on the palate. Sour cherry, a red-fruit quality, high astringency. The acidity is the point — it cuts through fatty food completely. Dry, persistent finish.

**Aromas**
Sour cherry, red currant, earth, dried herbs, a slight metallic mineral note.

**Aging**
3–7 years. The acidity is a preservative; good examples hold well.

**Classic regions**
Colli Orientali del Friuli DOC (Italy). Very limited production.

**Food**
The acidity is the key to pairing: prosciutto, fatty salumi, liver preparations (*fegato alla veneziana*), oily fish. Think of it as a squeeze of lemon that also happens to be red wine.

### Modra Frankinja (Blaufränkisch)

**History & name**
*Modra Frankinja* means "blue Frankish grape" in Slovenian — *modra* (blue/dark) and *Frankinja* derived from *frank* or Frankish, possibly indicating origin in the Frankish territories of medieval central Europe. The same grape is called *Blaufränkisch* in Austria and Germany, *Kékfrankos* in Hungary, *Lemberger* in Germany and the USA, *Frankovka* in Czech Republic and Slovakia. It is one of the few central European red grapes with genuine international recognition, primarily through Austrian Burgenland.

**Style**
Medium body. Firm, structured tannin. High acidity. Medium alcohol. Well-balanced rather than extreme in any direction.

**Taste**
Dark cherry, blackberry, peppery spice on the palate. Structured finish with good tannin grip but nothing harsh. Clean and food-friendly. Austrian examples (Burgenland) are typically more refined than Slovenian ones, though quality Slovenian examples (Batič, Dveri-Pax) are excellent.

**Aromas**
Dark cherry, blackberry, black pepper, dried herbs, sometimes a violet note. Earthy in Podravje styles; more structured and spicy in Austrian examples.

**Aging**
4–10 years for quality examples. Austrian Blaufränkisch from top estates (Moric, Pittnauer, Paul Achs) can age 15+ years.

**Classic regions**
Podravje region, Štajerska (Slovenia). Burgenland, especially Mittelburgenland (Austria) — the finest expressions. Smaller plantings in Hungary, Germany, Czech Republic.

**Food**
Goulash, pork schnitzel, roasted game, beef stew, grilled sausages. Particularly at home with central European and Balkan cuisine.

## Part 2: Major Italian Grapes

These grapes are widely available in local shops, appear frequently on restaurant lists, and give you the broader Italian context for understanding where FVG sits within Italian wine culture.

---

### Sangiovese

**History & name**
Italy's most widely planted red grape and the backbone of Tuscany. The name is traditionally said to derive from *Sanguis Jovis* — "blood of Jupiter" — though modern linguists consider this a 16th-century folk etymology rather than the true origin. More likely named after a place. Its exact origins are disputed; DNA analysis suggests it is native to central Italy, possibly Tuscany or Calabria. First unambiguous written reference dates to the 16th century in Tuscany.

**Style**
Medium-to-full body. High tannin. Very high acidity — one of the highest of any major red grape. Medium alcohol (12–14%).

**Taste**
Sour cherry, a distinctive savoury/tomato-skin note that few other reds have, dried herb finish. The acidity is the first thing you notice — mouth-watering, almost aggressive. Dry with firm tannin. Great examples have enormous depth.

**Aromas**
Sour cherry, dried cherry, tomato leaf, dried herbs (oregano, thyme), leather, earth, tobacco in aged wines.

**Aging**
Chianti Classico: 5–10 years. Brunello di Montalcino: minimum 8–15 years; exceptional vintages 25+ years.

**Classic regions**
Chianti Classico DOCG, Brunello di Montalcino DOCG, Vino Nobile di Montepulciano DOCG, Morellino di Scansano DOC (all Tuscany). Also significant in Umbria (Montefalco Rosso) and elsewhere in central Italy.

**Synonyms**
Brunello (Montalcino), Prugnolo Gentile (Montepulciano), Morellino (Maremma).

**Food**
Everything Italian with tomato sauce. Bistecca fiorentina (the Tuscan T-bone) is the classic pairing. Pizza, pasta al ragù, wild boar, aged Pecorino.

---

### Nebbiolo

**History & name**
Named from the Italian *nebbia* — fog. The grape is harvested late in autumn when the hills of Piedmont are shrouded in fog. First documented in Piedmont in 1268. DNA analysis confirms it is indigenous to the Piedmontese hills. Nebbiolo expresses terroir with extreme sensitivity — adjacent vineyards in Barolo can taste dramatically different, which is why the *MGA* (Menzione Geografica Aggiuntiva) system of 181 recognized vineyard sites in Barolo was introduced in 2010.

**Style**
Full body. Very high tannin — one of the most tannic grapes in the world. Very high acidity. High alcohol (typically 14–15%). Pale garnet color despite its power — one of the paradoxes of the grape.

**Taste**
In youth: iron, dried rose, tart cherry, massive tannin. Nearly unpleasant when young. With age: extraordinary complexity — tar, rose, dried fruit, leather, tobacco, earth, truffle. The tannin becomes silky. The finish can last 45+ seconds in great examples.

**Aromas**
Rose, dried rose, tar, cherry, tobacco, licorice, dried violet (young). Leather, truffle, coffee, dried herbs (aged).

**Aging**
Young Barolo is not meant to be drunk. Minimum 8 years before opening. Best at 15–25 years. Great vintages (2010, 2013, 2016) are still developing at 30 years.

**Classic regions**
Barolo DOCG and Barbaresco DOCG (Piedmont, Italy) — the two greatest expressions. Also Langhe Nebbiolo DOC (more accessible, drink younger), Gattinara DOCG, Ghemme DOCG (northern Piedmont), Valtellina (Lombardy).

**Food**
White truffle — the classic pairing, especially with tajarin pasta. Braised beef in Barolo (*brasato al Barolo*). Wild boar. Aged Parmigiano-Reggiano. Not a wine for light food.

---

### Primitivo / Zinfandel

**History & name**
The same grape, three names. In Croatia it is *Tribidrag* or *Crljenak Kaštelanski* — DNA analysis in 2002 confirmed this is the grape's origin, from the Dalmatian coast. It arrived in Puglia (southern Italy) where it became *Primitivo* — from *primaticcio* (early-ripening), because it is one of the first red grapes to ripen. It arrived in California in the mid-19th century via eastern US nurseries and became *Zinfandel*, considered an American original until DNA testing proved otherwise.

**Style**
Full body. Medium-high tannin. Medium-low acidity. High alcohol (often 14–16% — handle carefully).

**Taste**
Jammy, ripe dark fruit on the palate — blackberry jam, dried plum, sometimes a raisin quality. Warm and generous. The low acidity means it can feel slightly heavy without food.

**Aromas**
Blackberry, dried fruit, plum, black pepper, sometimes chocolate or coffee with oak aging.

**Aging**
Drink within 3–7 years. Primitivo di Manduria DOC and quality Zinfandels can hold 8–10 years but rarely improve dramatically.

**Classic regions**
Puglia, Italy — particularly Primitivo di Manduria DOC (near Taranto) and Gioia del Colle DOC. Napa Valley, Sonoma (Dry Creek Valley), Paso Robles (California) as Zinfandel.

**Food**
Barbecue, pizza, grilled sausage, spicy food, pasta al forno. The richness needs richness in return.

---

### Pinot Grigio

**History & name**
A colour mutation of Pinot Noir — the grape skin is grayish-pink (*grigio* = gray in Italian) rather than deep red or yellow. The mutation is ancient, known in Burgundy and Alsace for centuries. It arrived in northeastern Italy (Friuli, Alto Adige) in the 19th century via German-speaking settlers in the Alpine areas. In Italy it became one of the most commercially successful white wines in the world — unfortunately mostly in the form of cheap, neutral, mass-produced wine that bears little resemblance to what the grape can actually do. Serious Pinot Grigio from FVG is a corrective to that reputation.

**Style**
Light-to-medium body. High acidity. Bone dry. Low-medium alcohol (12–13%).

**Taste**
At its best (FVG, quality producers): precise and mineral with real texture, clean citrus and white peach, a long and dry finish. At its worst (commercial): thin, watery, barely present. The same grape, opposite results — producer matters enormously here.

**Aromas**
Lemon, white peach, pear, mineral. Restrained rather than aromatic — this is not a perfumed grape.

**Aging**
Most Pinot Grigio: drink within 2 years. Quality FVG examples (Vie di Romans Dessimis, Miani): 4–8 years, gaining complexity without losing freshness.

**Classic regions**
Friuli Isonzo DOC — Vie di Romans is the Italian benchmark. Collio DOC, Alto Adige DOC. Also Veneto (large-volume commercial). Oregon (USA) produces interesting examples.

**Food**
Seafood pasta, grilled fish, pizza bianca, mild cheeses, aperitivo, risotto di mare.

---

### Pinot Gris

**History & name**
The same grape as Pinot Grigio — a colour mutation of Pinot Noir — but made in a completely different style, primarily in Alsace (France) and Oregon (USA). The French name *Pinot Gris* became associated with a richer, more aromatic, often off-dry interpretation. In Alsace it was historically called Tokay d'Alsace (same EU naming dispute as Friulano — the name was banned in 2007). The style is so different from Italian Pinot Grigio that knowing they are the same grape is genuinely surprising to most wine drinkers.

**Style**
Full body. Lower acidity than Pinot Grigio. Often off-dry to medium-sweet. High alcohol (13.5–14.5%). Rich and textured.

**Taste**
Rich and slightly viscous on the palate. Honey, ripe stone fruit, warming spice. The residual sweetness (where present) is balanced by the grape's inherent richness rather than sharp acidity. Long, warming finish.

**Aromas**
Honey, apricot, quince, white spice (ginger, cinnamon), sometimes smoke. Much more aromatic and expressive than Pinot Grigio.

**Aging**
Alsatian Pinot Gris: 5–12 years. Grand Cru examples can age 20 years.

**Classic regions**
Alsace (France) — the world benchmark, where Grand Cru Pinot Gris from sites like Rangen or Schlossberg is world-class. Oregon (USA) for a drier, more restrained style.

**Food**
Foie gras (the classic Alsatian pairing), pork roast, smoked duck, washed-rind cheese, dishes with spice or smoke.

---

### Gewürztraminer

**History & name**
*Gewürz* means "spice" in German. *Traminer* refers to Tramin (Termeno in Italian), a village in Alto Adige, South Tyrol — where the grape is believed to have originated and from where it spread through central Europe. So the full name means "spicy Traminer." The variety has been cultivated in Alto Adige since at least the Middle Ages. Alsace became its most famous home. It is one of the most immediately recognizable wines in the world — the perfumed aroma is unlike any other grape.

**Style**
Full body. Low acidity. Dry to slightly off-dry. High alcohol (13–14%).

**Taste**
Rich and slightly oily texture. Rosewater, lychee, warming spice on the palate. The low acidity means it can feel heavy without food. A polarizing wine — people tend to love it or find it overwhelming.

**Aromas**
Rose petal, lychee, Turkish delight, ginger, clove. Extremely perfumed — you can often smell it across the room. Unmistakable.

**Aging**
3–8 years. Alsatian Grand Cru examples can age 15+ years.

**Classic regions**
Alsace (France) — the benchmark. Alto Adige DOC (Italy, as Traminer Aromatico) — often more restrained and elegant than Alsace. Germany, New Zealand.

**Food**
Spicy Asian cuisine (Thai, Vietnamese, Indian). Foie gras. Blue cheese. Washed-rind cheese (Munster with Gewürztraminer is the classic Alsatian combination). Avoid delicate or subtle food — the wine overwhelms it.

## Part 3: International Reference Grapes

These grapes are available in every wine shop across Europe and the world. You will encounter them on every restaurant list. Learn them as reference points — they give you a global vocabulary and comparison benchmarks when tasting local wines.

---

### Cabernet Sauvignon

**History & name**
The world's most planted red grape. DNA analysis revealed in 1997 that it is a natural cross of Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc — a chance crossing that occurred in 17th-century Bordeaux. The name combines the parent names. It dominated Bordeaux's Left Bank for centuries before spreading globally in the 20th century.

**Style**
Full body. High tannin. High acidity. High alcohol (13.5–15%).

**Taste**
Blackcurrant, dark fruit, cedar, firm tannic grip that coats the entire mouth. The finish is long and dry with persistent tannin. Young examples can be harsh; age softens them into something sophisticated.

**Aromas**
Blackcurrant, black cherry, cedar, graphite, tobacco, pencil shavings. Bell pepper or mint when underripe (a sign of poor growing conditions).

**Aging**
Needs time. 5–15 years for good examples. Great Bordeaux or Napa Cabernet: 20–30 years.

**Classic regions**
Bordeaux Left Bank — Médoc, Pauillac, Margaux, Saint-Julien (France). Napa Valley (California). Bolgheri (Tuscany — Super Tuscans). Maipo Valley (Chile).

**Food**
Red meat, lamb, aged hard cheese. Nothing delicate — this wine overpowers subtle food.

---

### Merlot

**History & name**
Named from the French *merle* — blackbird, which reportedly loves to eat the ripe grapes. Documented in Bordeaux's Libournais region (Right Bank) from the early 18th century. Became one of the world's most widely planted grapes due to its approachability — softer than Cabernet, earlier-drinking. Pétrus, the world's most expensive Merlot (€2,000–5,000 per bottle), is almost 100% Merlot.

**Style**
Medium-to-full body. Softer tannin than Cabernet. Medium-high acidity. Medium-high alcohol.

**Taste**
Plum, dark cherry, roundness on the palate. Less grip than Cabernet. Smooth finish. Easy to understand even when young.

**Aromas**
Plum, red cherry, chocolate, sometimes a herbal note, vanilla from oak aging.

**Aging**
Good Merlot: 5–12 years. Great Right Bank Bordeaux (Pétrus, Pomerol): 15–25 years.

**Classic regions**
Bordeaux Right Bank — Pomerol, Saint-Émilion (France). California, Washington State, Chile. Also common in northern Italy (Collio, COF, Veneto) as a blending component and increasingly a varietal wine.

**Food**
Duck, roast pork, pasta with meat sauce, mild game, aged cheese.

---

### Pinot Noir

**History & name**
*Pinot* from the French for pine cone — the tightly clustered grape bunches. *Noir* means black. Documented in Burgundy since at least the 14th century; probably cultivated there much earlier. It is one of the oldest cultivated varieties in Europe. Its difficulty — sensitive to disease, difficult to ripen, highly reflective of terroir — makes it the grape that separates great winemakers from ordinary ones.

**Style**
Light-to-medium body. Low tannin. High acidity. Low-medium alcohol (12–13.5%). Pale garnet color.

**Taste**
Red cherry, strawberry, a delicate satin texture in the mouth. The finish, in great examples, is very long despite the apparent lightness. Silky. One of the few red wines that doesn't feel heavy even after a glass.

**Aromas**
Red cherry, raspberry, rose petal (young). Earth, mushroom, forest floor, leather, dried rose (aged Burgundy — the so-called *sous-bois* character).

**Aging**
Village Burgundy: 5–10 years. Premier Cru: 10–20 years. Grand Cru: 15–30 years. Oregon/NZ Pinot: 5–12 years.

**Classic regions**
Burgundy — Côte de Nuits (France) for the greatest reds. Willamette Valley (Oregon, USA). Central Otago, Martinborough (New Zealand). Champagne (blended into sparkling). Germany (as Spätburgunder).

**Food**
Salmon, duck, mushroom risotto, charcuterie, Gruyère and mild aged cheeses.

---

### Syrah

**History & name**
DNA analysis in 1999 confirmed Syrah is a natural cross of two obscure French grapes — Dureza and Mondeuse Blanche — from the northern Rhône valley of France. There is no Persian origin, despite the persistent legend connecting it to the city of Shiraz in Iran; the name similarity is coincidental and has no genetic basis. Syrah was first documented in the Rhône valley in the early 17th century and remained a regional specialty until the late 20th century, when its reputation spread globally.

**Style**
Full body. High tannin. Medium-high acidity. Medium-high alcohol (13–14%). Cool-climate style — more savory and restrained than its Australian counterpart.

**Taste**
Dark fruit (blackberry, black cherry) underpinned by black pepper, olive brine, and smoked meat. Dense and savory on the palate. The finish is long with an earthy, almost meaty quality. Less overtly fruity than warm-climate Shiraz.

**Aromas**
Black pepper (very pronounced), violet, olive, dark fruit, smoked meat. Aged northern Rhône Syrah develops leather, graphite, and dried meat notes.

**Aging**
Northern Rhône benchmarks (Hermitage, Côte-Rôtie): 10–25 years. The wines are almost impenetrable young; patience is rewarded with extraordinary complexity.

**Classic regions**
Northern Rhône Valley — Hermitage AOC and Côte-Rôtie AOC (France) are the world benchmarks. Also Crozes-Hermitage, Saint-Joseph, Cornas. Growing presence in Tuscany (IGT blends), California, and South Africa.

**Food**
Lamb, game birds, venison, hard aged cheese. Northern Rhône Syrah is one of the great companions for braised lamb.

---

### Shiraz

**History & name**
Shiraz is not a different grape from Syrah — it is the Australian name for the same variety, adopted when French Syrah cuttings arrived in Australia in the 1830s, brought by James Busby. Over nearly two centuries of warm-climate viticulture in South Australia, the style diverged so dramatically from its French origins that the wines are now treated as essentially different experiences. The name *Shiraz* has become synonymous with the warm-climate, full-bodied, oak-influenced style.

**Style**
Full body. High tannin. Medium acidity. High alcohol (14–15.5%, sometimes more). Warm-climate style — richer, darker, and more opulent than French Syrah.

**Taste**
Blackberry jam, dark plum, chocolate, a vanilla and spice quality from oak aging. Warm, generous finish. The fruit is primary and forward — very different from the savory restraint of northern Rhône Syrah. Old-vine Barossa Shiraz adds dried fruit, leather, and enormous concentration.

**Aromas**
Dark fruit jam, chocolate, coffee, vanilla oak, sometimes eucalyptus (a distinctive Australian Shiraz marker), mocha.

**Aging**
Quality Barossa Shiraz: 8–20 years. Old-vine single-vineyard examples (Penfolds Grange, Henschke Hill of Grace) can age 30+ years.

**Classic regions**
Barossa Valley (South Australia) — the benchmark, home to some of the oldest Shiraz vines in the world, some over 150 years old. McLaren Vale, Clare Valley, Hunter Valley (NSW). Also important in Washington State (USA) and South Africa.

**Food**
Barbecue, grilled sausages, beef ribs, blue cheese, dark chocolate desserts. The richness needs richness in return.

---

### Grenache / Garnacha / Cannonau

**History & name**
Originated in Spain, where it is called *Garnacha*. DNA studies suggest its origin may be in the Kingdom of Aragon (northern Spain). It spread through southern France (*Grenache*) and to Sardinia (*Cannonau*) during the period of Spanish dominance over those territories (15th–17th centuries). One of the most widely planted grapes in the world.

**Style**
Medium-to-full body. Low tannin. Medium acidity. High alcohol potential (often 14–15.5%).

**Taste**
Red fruit — raspberry, strawberry, dried cherry. Warmth from the alcohol. Soft tannin. Herbs and garrigue in the finish. Generous and round.

**Aromas**
Raspberry, strawberry, dried herbs, white pepper, sometimes orange peel or dried rose.

**Aging**
3–8 years for most examples. Old-vine Grenache (Priorat, Châteauneuf-du-Pape) can age 15+ years.

**Classic regions**
Southern Rhône — Châteauneuf-du-Pape (France). Priorat, Aragon, Navarra (Spain). Sardinia as Cannonau DOC (Italy). Southern Australia.

**Food**
Mediterranean food: lamb, slow-cooked meat, paella, tapas. Sardinian Cannonau with lamb and roasted meats is a regional classic.

---

### Tempranillo

**History & name**
Spain's most important indigenous grape. The name comes from *temprano* — early — because it ripens earlier than most Spanish varieties. DNA analysis confirms it is native to the Iberian Peninsula. It has been cultivated there for at least 3,000 years. In Portugal, where it is called *Aragonez* or *Tinta Roriz*, it plays a key role in Port wine blends.

**Style**
Medium body. Medium tannin. Medium acidity. Medium alcohol.

**Taste**
Cherry, leather, vanilla (from the oak aging typical of Rioja). Approachable even young; complex when aged. Crianza and Reserva styles show increasing depth and complexity.

**Aromas**
Cherry, plum, dried fig, vanilla (oak), leather, tobacco.

**Aging**
Joven: drink young. Crianza: 3–6 years. Reserva: 5–10 years. Gran Reserva: 8–15 years.

**Classic regions**
Rioja DOCa and Ribera del Duero DO (Spain) — the two greatest expressions. Also Toro, La Mancha. Portugal: Douro, Dão (as Aragones/Tinta Roriz).

**Synonyms**
Tinto Fino (Ribera del Duero), Tinto de Toro (Toro), Aragonez / Tinta Roriz (Portugal).

**Food**
Lamb chops, roast meats, manchego cheese, tapas.

---

### Chardonnay

**History & name**
Named after the village of Chardonnay in the Mâconnais area of Burgundy. DNA analysis confirmed it is a natural cross of Pinot Noir and an obscure grape called Gouais Blanc. It is the world's most widely planted white grape and the most influential — it set the template for what expensive white wine should taste and smell like during the global wine boom of the 1980s–90s.

**Style**
Wide range depending on winemaking. Unoaked: light-to-medium body, high acidity, clean. Heavily oaked: full body, lower apparent acidity, rich texture.

**Taste**
Unoaked (Chablis, stainless steel): crisp, apple, citrus, mineral, clean finish.
Oaked (white Burgundy, California): rich, creamy, buttery finish, vanilla, longer and more complex.

**Aromas**
Unoaked: green apple, lemon, chalk, sometimes flint. Oaked/warm climate: butter, vanilla, toast, tropical fruit, peach.

**Aging**
Entry-level: drink within 3 years. Grand Cru white Burgundy: 15–25 years.

**Classic regions**
Burgundy — Chablis, Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet, Chassagne-Montrachet (France). Champagne (for Blanc de Blancs). California (Napa, Sonoma). Also Alto Adige and Friuli (Italy) for well-made varietal examples.

**Food**
Unoaked: oysters, grilled fish, light seafood. Oaked: lobster, scallops, cream sauces, roast chicken.

---

### Sauvignon Blanc

**History & name**
*Sauvignon* derives from the French *sauvage* — wild — possibly referring to the grape's intensely pungent, herbaceous aromas, or to its tendency to grow vigorously in the wild. It originated in the Loire Valley of France. DNA analysis shows it is a parent of Cabernet Sauvignon (crossed with Cabernet Franc).

**Style**
Light-to-medium body. Very high acidity. Bone dry. Low-medium alcohol.

**Taste**
Sharp, refreshing acidity. Citrus and green fruit on the palate. Very clean finish. The herbaceous quality varies by region — more mineral and restrained in France, more intensely herbal and tropical in New Zealand.

**Aromas**
Grapefruit, lime, green pepper, cut grass, gooseberry, nettle. Sometimes described as "cat's pee" — more appetizing than it sounds. One of the most immediately recognizable grapes.

**Aging**
Drink young — 1–4 years. Not intended for aging in most cases.

**Classic regions**
Loire Valley — Sancerre, Pouilly-Fumé (France, mineral and restrained). Marlborough (New Zealand, intensely herbaceous). Bordeaux (blended with Sémillon). Friuli (FVG — serious varietal examples from Collio and Isonzo).

**Food**
Goat cheese (Sancerre with chèvre is the French classic). Asparagus, green salads, grilled white fish, oysters.

---

### Riesling

**History & name**
The name appears in German records as early as 1435, referring to a vine in the Rheingau. Its exact origin is debated; DNA analysis suggests it is native to Germany. *Riesling* is likely derived from an old German word, possibly related to *reißen* (to rush or tear) or simply a place name. It is considered by many wine experts to be the greatest white grape in the world — capable of expressing terroir with more precision than almost any other variety.

**Style**
Light body. Very high acidity. Low alcohol (7–10% in German styles; 12–14% in Alsatian dry styles). Can be bone dry or intensely sweet — the label must be read carefully.

**Taste**
Dry Riesling: racy acidity, citrus and mineral on the palate, very clean and precise finish, sometimes a petrol/kerosene note that develops with age (considered positive).
Sweet Riesling: intense sweetness balanced by equally intense acidity — never cloying. Apricot, honey, and the same petrol note.

**Aromas**
Lime, green apple, slate, petrol/kerosene (aged), apricot (sweeter styles), white flowers.

**Aging**
One of the longest-lived white grapes. Dry Riesling: 5–15 years. Sweet German BA/TBA: 20–50 years. The petrol character develops and becomes a prized complexity marker.

**Classic regions**
Mosel (Germany) — the most elegant, lowest alcohol, most age-worthy. Rheingau (Germany). Wachau, Kamptal (Austria) for serious dry styles. Alsace (France) for fuller dry styles.

**Food**
Dry: spicy food (Thai, Indian, Chinese), pork, duck, sushi, smoked salmon.
Sweet: foie gras, blue cheese, fruit desserts.

---

### Albariño / Alvarinho

**History & name**
*Alba* = white, *riño* = riverine or vine-related in Galician Portuguese. An old variety from the wet, Atlantic corner of the Iberian peninsula — Galicia (northwest Spain) and the Minho region of Portugal. Some theories suggest it arrived from the Rhine region with Burgundian monks in the 12th century, though this is debated. It became internationally known only in the 1990s when Rías Baixas DO was established.

**Style**
Light-to-medium body. Very high acidity. Saline mineral quality. Low-medium alcohol.

**Taste**
Lively and precise. Citrus and white peach, then a saline, almost sea-spray quality on the finish. Very refreshing, almost bracing.

**Aromas**
Citrus peel, peach, apricot, white flowers, saline mineral note.

**Aging**
Drink within 2–4 years. Not intended for aging.

**Classic regions**
Rías Baixas DO (Galicia, Spain). Vinho Verde DOC (Portugal, as Alvarinho — slightly lighter and lower alcohol).

**Food**
Atlantic seafood: octopus, clams, barnacles, grilled fish. The classic Galician combination is pulpo a feira (octopus) with Albariño. Also excellent with Adriatic seafood.

## Self-Quiz

1. What is Friulano called in Slovenia, and why was it renamed?
2. What does the name "Zelen" mean, and what does it tell you about the wine?
3. Grenache, Garnacha, and Cannonau are three names for the same grape. Match each name to its country or region of use.
4. Ribolla Gialla made as a conventional white wine and as an orange wine — what are the key differences in taste and what food does each version suit?
5. Why is Teran protected as a PDO in Slovenia, and what makes it different from ordinary Refosco?
6. Cabernet Sauvignon is described as high tannin, high acidity. What does high tannin feel like physically in the mouth?
7. Riesling ranges from bone dry to very sweet. If you see a German Riesling label that says "Spätlese" but no other sweetness indication — what should you expect?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. In Slovenia, Friulano is called Jakot — Tocai spelled backwards. It was renamed because the EU banned the name "Tocai Friulano" in 2007 to avoid confusion with Hungarian Tokaji, despite no historical link between the two. The Slovenian winemakers adopted the backwards spelling as a pointed response to the ruling.

2. Zelen means "green" in Slovenian. It tells you the wine will have a fresh, herbal, grassy character — the name directly describes the wine's most distinctive quality.

3. Grenache — France (southern Rhône, Languedoc). Garnacha — Spain (Aragon, Rioja, Priorat). Cannonau — Sardinia, Italy. All three are the same grape; the names reflect the centuries of Spanish political influence over both southern France and Sardinia, which is how the grape spread from its Spanish origin.

4. Conventional Ribolla: high acidity, citrus, green apple, light and refreshing — pairs with seafood and light pasta. Orange wine Ribolla: amber color, tannin, dried apricot, walnut, chamomile, long complex finish — pairs like a light red wine with pork, aged cheese, charcuterie.

5. Teran was protected as a PDO (PDO = Zaščitena označba porekla in Slovenian) because Italy wanted to use the name for a different wine in the Carso DOC. Slovenia won the EU dispute, establishing Teran as exclusively Slovenian Kras. As a wine, Teran is denser, more purple-black, and higher in polyphenols than standard Refosco — the Kras limestone soils give it an additional iron-mineral intensity.

6. Tannin creates a drying, astringent sensation — your gums and the inside of your cheeks feel parched and slightly rough, as if you've eaten an unripe banana or strong black tea. It is a textural feeling, not a taste.

7. Spätlese without "Trocken" on the label traditionally means off-dry to medium-sweet — the grapes were picked late (riper), and the wine was not fermented to full dryness. If it said "Spätlese Trocken," then despite the late harvest the wine was fermented dry. Always look for "Trocken" if you want dry German Riesling.

</details>

## Practical Exercise

Your first five bottles to buy and taste, in order — these are all available locally:

1. **Friulano** — any Collio or COF producer, current vintage. Your local white reference point.
2. **Ribolla Gialla** — conventional style, from Klet Brda, Edi Simčič, or similar. Compare with the Friulano: what's different?
3. **Vitovska** — any Carso/Kras producer. The most unusual white on this list. Notice the mineral and saline character.
4. **Refosco or Teran** — your local red reference point. Pair it with something fatty (prosciutto, aged cheese) and note how the wine changes.
5. **Riesling** — German Mosel, Spätlese (slight sweetness). This gives you a global comparison point for acidity and shows how a completely different style handles the same structural element.

For each: write three lines before opening (expected aromas, taste, style) and three lines after. The gap between prediction and reality is where learning happens.
