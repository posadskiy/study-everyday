# Module 7 — Wine Quality & Price

Why does a bottle of Château Pétrus cost €3,000? Why does a Slovenian Rebula from a great producer cost €15? And why does a €50 supermarket wine sometimes taste worse than a €12 wine from a good shop?

This module explains the actual drivers of wine price and quality — and how to navigate the gap between the two.

---

## What Actually Makes Wine Expensive

Price and quality are related but not the same thing. Price is set by the market. Quality is set by what's in the glass. They often diverge.

### 1. Land Value and Scarcity

The most powerful price driver.

**Romanée-Conti** (Domaine de la Romanée-Conti): 1.8 hectares of Grand Cru Burgundy. The vineyard alone is worth over €1 billion. Annual production: roughly 6,000 bottles. Global demand: vastly higher. Price: €15,000–25,000+ per bottle. This is not primarily about quality (though the wine is extraordinary) — it is about extreme scarcity and trophy status.

**Pétrus** (Pomerol): 11.5 hectares on the famous clay plateau. Around 30,000 bottles per year. Price: €2,000–5,000 per bottle.

**Gaja Barbaresco**: 100 hectares in prime Piedmont. Much larger production. Price: €200–500. Still exceptional, but the equation shifts.

The pattern: **smaller production + more famous area + higher demand = higher price**.

Many excellent wines come from less famous regions where land is affordable and producers can make extraordinary wine at reasonable prices.

### 2. Low Yields

The amount of fruit produced per hectare of vineyard directly affects quality. More grapes per vine = more dilute. The plant's energy is spread across more fruit.

- Top Burgundy Grand Cru: 30–35 hl/ha (hectoliters per hectare)
- Typical regional Burgundy: 50–55 hl/ha
- High-volume Languedoc: 80–100 hl/ha
- Irrigation-heavy industrial wine: 150+ hl/ha

Low yields require either:
- Green harvesting (cutting off grape bunches in summer to concentrate remaining fruit — expensive labor)
- Old vines (naturally produce less fruit; old vines are also more resilient to variation)
- Appellation rules that legally restrict yields

### 3. Old Vines

Vines older than 25–30 years are considered "old vines" (though no legal minimum exists). Older vines:
- Have deeper root systems, accessing water and minerals more reliably
- Produce fewer but more concentrated grapes naturally
- May develop more complex flavors from decades of root exploration through specific soils

The oldest commercial vineyards are in Australia (Barossa Valley: vines planted in the 1840s survive because Australia escaped Phylloxera). Some Bordeaux châteaux have blocks of centenarian vines.

### 4. Aging and Storage Costs

Every year a wine sits in barrel or bottle, it costs the producer money:
- **Barrel aging:** A new French oak barrel costs €700–1,200. Replace it every 1–3 years. Barolo requires 2 years minimum in wood — these barrels tie up capital
- **Bottle aging before release:** Barolo DOCG: minimum 3 years before release (5 for Riserva). Brunello: minimum 5 years. This capital is locked up in inventory
- **Warehouse/cellar space:** Physical storage costs money

A wine aged 5 years before release carries 5 years of carrying costs that a "new release" wine does not.

### 5. Labor-Intensive Production

Prestige estates often hand-harvest, hand-sort twice (vineyard + winery), and manage every aspect manually:
- Hand-punching down the cap during fermentation
- Minimal intervention in the cellar (no fining, filtration)
- Individual barrel selection and blending
- Hand-labeling and waxing

All of this is expensive. Industrial wine uses mechanical harvesting, centrifuges, and minimal human labor per bottle.

### 6. Critic Scores and Brand Reputation

Robert Parker's 100-point scores (and now various successors) demonstrably move market prices. A wine scoring 97+ Parker points can double or triple in price within months of the score's publication.

**The Matthew Effect in wine:** Famous estates get premium prices partly because they are famous. Château Mouton Rothschild is not necessarily worth 10× a well-made Pauillac "second wine" on a pure quality basis. Much of the premium is brand value.

### 7. Packaging

- **Heavy bottles:** Some producers use 900g+ glass bottles. This adds cost and implies (without guaranteeing) quality
- **Complex capsules, embossed labels, wooden boxes:** Cost money but don't affect the wine

## The Price/Quality Curve

The relationship between price and quality is **not linear**.

```
Quality
  │
  │                                    ●  (icon wines)
  │                           ●  ●
  │                  ●  ●  ●
  │         ●  ●  ●
  │   ●  ●
  │●
  └──────────────────────────────────── Price
    €5  €10  €20  €40  €80  €150  €500+
```

In the €5–20 range, quality increases meaningfully with price. In the €20–60 range, quality improves but more slowly. Above €60–80, much of the price premium is brand, scarcity, and trophy value rather than perceptible quality.

**The most value lives in the €15–40 range** from producers in regions where land is affordable but skill is high: Slovenia, Friuli (sub-€40 wines), Languedoc, Southern Italy, Galicia, Ribera del Duero below Vega Sicilia, Oregon (mid-tier), Austria.

## Understanding Critics and Scores

### The 100-Point Scale (Robert Parker / Wine Spectator / Wine Advocate)

- 96–100: Extraordinary
- 90–95: Outstanding to exceptional
- 85–89: Very good to excellent
- 80–84: Good
- Below 80: Not recommended

**Practical reality:** Wine publications rarely print scores below 80 — they buy bottles at retail and don't review wines they dislike. Anything over 87–88 from a respected critic is genuinely good. The difference between 94 and 96 points is often marginal and highly subjective.

**Parker's influence is waning** as wine culture diversifies. New generation critics (Jancis Robinson, Antonio Galloni, Eric Asimov, Michelin-starred restaurant lists) have broader aesthetic values.

### Jancis Robinson (20-point scale)

The world's most respected wine critic. Uses the 20-point scale (see Module 6). Her reviews are known for honesty, including pointing out wines she doesn't like regardless of prestige.

### Wine Spectator / Decanter / Wine & Spirits / Vinous

Major publications. All use variations of the 100-point scale. Cover different markets and styles. Cross-reference multiple sources before buying based on scores alone.

### The Problem with Scores

- **Blind tasting conditions differ:** A wine tasted at a competition with 200 others is different from the same wine at dinner
- **Palate preferences vary:** Parker historically preferred riper, oak-forward, high-alcohol reds. This influenced which styles scored highest during his peak influence (1990–2010)
- **Scores don't indicate value:** A 90-point $200 wine may be worse value than a 88-point $20 wine
- **New release bias:** Critics taste young wines that will develop differently

Use scores as a starting filter, not a final verdict. A wine with consistent 90+ scores across multiple critics is more reliable than a single high score.

## Vintage Charts

Not all years are created equal. Vintage quality varies by region and year.

**Why vintages vary:**
- Growing season temperature (too cool = unripe; too hot = overripe)
- Rainfall timing (rain before harvest = dilution, rot; drought = stress on vines; spring rain = good)
- Disease pressure (mildew, botrytis — can be good or bad)
- Frost, hail: Can destroy entire crops

**How to use a vintage chart:** The [Vintage Chart reference sheet](../reference/vintage-chart.md) covers major regions. Use it when buying wines intended for aging — a great producer in a poor vintage can make a good wine, but may not be worth the full premium.

**General recent notable vintages:**

| Burgundy | 2010, 2015, 2019, 2020 | 2007, 2012, 2013 |
| Bordeaux | 2009, 2010, 2016, 2018 | 2011, 2013 |

| Collio/Brda (FVG/SI) | 2014, 2016, 2019, 2021 | 2009, 2017 (drought) |

## How to Spot Overpriced and Undervalued Wines

### Overpriced Signals

- Famous appellation + ordinary producer
- High Parker score from 15 years ago; wine hasn't been re-evaluated
- Restaurant mark-up on famous labels (restaurants mark up 3–4× retail)
- Heavy bottle, elaborate packaging without commensurate wine quality
- Wines from "hot" regions where land price has risen faster than quality
- Commercial brands with large advertising budgets

### Value Signals

- Less-famous appellations within famous regions (village Burgundy over Grand Cru; Barbera d'Asti over Barolo; Mâconnais Chardonnay over Meursault)
- Producer quality higher than appellation reputation (a great producer in a basic Languedoc AOC)
- Regions going through quality renaissance before prices catch up
- Old vines in unfashionable regions (old-vine Garnacha in Aragon; old-vine Chenin Blanc in Swartland)
- Grower Champagne vs. big house NV at same price
- Slovenian and FVG producers: quality/price ratio is excellent compared to equivalent Burgundy or Tuscany

### Best Value Regions (Currently)

| Region | Why Valuable |
|--------|-------------|
| Goriška Brda / Collio | World-class whites and orange wines at €10–30 |
| Vipava Valley | Indigenous grapes (Zelen, Pinela) at €8–20 |
| Languedoc-Roussillon | Former bulk wine region now producing serious wine |
| Sicilia (Etna especially) | Volcanic Nerello Mascalese before prices spike fully |
| Barbera d'Asti DOCG | Great Piedmont winemakers at a quarter of Barolo price |
| Southern Rhône (village level) | Côtes du Rhône Villages vs Châteauneuf premium |
| Swartland, South Africa | Old-vine Chenin Blanc world-class but cheap |
| Alentejo, Portugal | Full-bodied reds at €8–20 |
| Ribeira Sacra, Spain | Steep-slope Mencía, distinctive and affordable |

## What You're Not Paying For (in cheap wine)

Industrial-scale cheap wine achieves low price through:
- Machine harvesting, no sorting
- High yields (dilute fruit)
- Short fermentation at high temperature
- Oak chips or staves instead of barrels
- Blended from multiple regions, multiple vintages
- Flavoring agents, tartaric acid additions, Mega Purple (a grape concentrate that adds color and sweetness)
- Minimal cellar time

None of these automatically make a bad wine — but they do produce wine that has been optimized for price, not for character. Wine made this way rarely has complexity, finish, or any meaningful relationship to a place.

## Self-Quiz

1. Why does Romanée-Conti cost so much? Is it purely quality?
2. What is the difference between low-yield and high-yield viticulture and how does it affect the wine?
3. Why should you not use a single critic's score as the only basis for buying?
4. Name three value wine regions and explain why they offer good quality-to-price ratios.
5. What is "vintage variation" and why does it matter more in Burgundy than in Barossa Valley?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No — it's a combination of quality and extreme scarcity. The vineyard is 1.8 hectares producing roughly 6,000 bottles per year against global demand from collectors and restaurants worldwide. The land itself is worth over €1 billion. At that production volume, almost any price can be charged. The wine is genuinely extraordinary, but a significant part of the price is trophy and scarcity value.

2. Yield = how many grapes a vine produces per hectare. Low yield: the vine's energy concentrates into fewer grapes → more concentrated flavor, deeper color, more complex wine. High yield: the energy is spread across more grapes → dilute, thin wine. This is why appellation rules restrict yields and why "old vines" are valued — older vines produce less fruit naturally.

3. Critics have individual palate preferences that bias their scores (Parker historically preferred ripe, oak-forward reds — wines that differ from that style scored lower regardless of quality). Scores are also given under tasting conditions different from drinking at home. A wine scoring 90 from one critic might score 94 from another with different tastes. Cross-reference multiple sources and consider the price/score relationship, not score alone.

4. Any three of: Goriška Brda / Collio (world-class whites and orange wines at €10–30, unknown to most buyers); Languedoc (formerly bulk wine, now quality estates but land is cheap); Barbera d'Asti (Piedmont winemakers at a fraction of Barolo price); Swartland, South Africa (old-vine Chenin Blanc world-class but underpriced); Sicilia / Etna (rising quality, still affordable); Vipava Valley (unique indigenous grapes, very affordable).

In Burgundy (cool, marginal, continental climate), a rainy cold summer produces unripe grapes; a warm dry one produces exceptional wine — the difference is huge. This is why Burgundy collectors obsess over vintages and Barossa buyers barely check.

## Practical Exercise

**The value experiment:** Go to a good wine shop. Tell them your budget is €20 and you want the best-quality wine they have at that price. Buy what they recommend. Separately, buy a well-known brand in the same price range. Taste blind (ask a friend to pour without showing labels). Which one wins? The results are usually instructive about where value actually sits.
